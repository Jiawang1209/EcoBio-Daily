### The Implications of Microbial Functional Diversity on the Response of Soil Organic Matter Decomposition to Nitrogen Fertilization: A Theoretical Model

Abstract. Nitrogen (N) fertilization decreases carbon (C) decomposition, but there remain competing hypotheses underlying this response including reduced belowground C allocation, shifts in microbial diversity, and altered microbial function. Further, current soil organic matter (SOM) decomposition models lack the ability to test these hypothesized mechanisms. To theoretically test these mechanisms, we integrated the Carbon Acquisition Ecological Strategies (CAES) framework into the Carbon, Organisms, Rhizosphere, and Protection in the Soil Environment (CORPSE) model. CAES represents a pathway for integrating functional diversity into microbial explicit soil decomposition models, with three microbial functional groups that specialize in decomposing and utilizing a different SOM pool: primary decomposers (1° decomposers) target complex SOM, secondary decomposers (2° decomposers) target microbial necromass, and passive consumers target simple SOM. We then used the refined model (Functional Group-CORPSE) to theoretically test hypotheses by leveraging long term data from a 30 year, watershed scale N fertilization experiment at the Fernow Experimental Forest in Parsons, WV. We performed modeling experiments to evaluate the microbial community response to varying C and N inputs, and to simulate hypothesized mechanisms of microbial response to N deposition. Varying substrate composition led to distinct microbial communities, reflecting substrate preferences between functional groups. However, only when both the function and abundance of complex C decomposers was reduced could FG-CORPSE capture the SOM dynamics observed at the Fernow. Collectively, our results show that FG-CORPSE enables the simulation of both functional and compositional shifts in microbial communities to facilitate the testing of microbe centric hypotheses for decomposition and C storage under N enrichment.

### Nutrient Cycling Microbes in Legume Rhizosphere

Legumes play a crucial role in sustainable agriculture through their symbiotic association with nitrogen-fixing rhizobia and other nutrient-solubilising microorganisms. These interactions enable legumes to fix atmospheric nitrogen, mobilise phosphorus, potassium and micronutrients, enrich soil organic matter, and improve soil structure, thereby reducing the need for synthetic fertilisers. In addition, rhizosphere microbes produce growth-promoting compounds and confer tolerance to environmental stresses, while mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi contribute to nutrient mobilisation, litter decomposition, and soil carbon sequestration. This chapter highlights the legume rhizosphere as a unique microbial niche, emphasising its ecological complexity and role in nutrient cycling. It explores plant–microbial interactions and functional networks that underpin legume productivity and soil health. The discussion extends to environmental and agronomic factors that influence microbial diversity and function within the rhizosphere, followed by insights into biotechnological and ecological applications of legume-associated microbes for sustainable intensification. Finally, the chapter outlines current challenges and future perspectives, focusing on harnessing legume–microbe interactions to enhance nutrient use efficiency, promote soil restoration, and develop climate-resilient agricultural systems.

### Effect of Chemical Fertilisers on Microbiome in Legumes

This chapter synthesises recent progress in understanding how chemical fertilisers modulate the soil microbiome, with particular emphasis on legume-based production systems. Inputs of mineral nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium provide readily available nutrients that stimulate plant growth, increase root biomass, and enhance the return of crop residues thereby influencing soil organic matter dynamics. Soil microbial communities exhibit high sensitivity to fertilisation-induced shifts in nutrient availability, often showing measurable changes in population abundance and functional activity even when overall taxonomic diversity appears relatively stable. However, excessive or prolonged fertiliser application can diminish key soil enzymatic activities, accelerate the depletion of organic matter, and promote soil acidification. These physicochemical changes lead to marked alterations in both bacterial and fungal community composition. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of balanced and judicious fertiliser management to sustain crop productivity while preserving soil microbial integrity and long-term soil fertility.

### Size-Dependent Mechanism of Selenium Nanoparticles in Regulating Cadmium Accumulation in the Soil–Rice (Oryza sativa L.) System

Selenium nanoparticles (Se NPs) have shown potential for regulating cadmium (Cd) accumulation in rice; however, their particle-size-dependent effects in the paddy soil–rice system remain unclear. In this study, a whole-growth-period pot experiment was conducted to investigate the mechanisms of Se NPs with different particle sizes (50, 100, and 200 nm) applied at different rates (20 and 50 mg/kg) in regulating Cd accumulation in rice grown in Cd-contaminated paddy soil. Se NP application significantly altered rhizosphere pH and redox potential, decreased Cd concentrations in soil solution and DTPA-extractable Cd, and increased soil solution Se and KH2PO4-extractable Se. In the 20 mg/kg treatment, Se NPs promoted the transformation of Cd from exchangeable fractions to Fe–Mn oxide-bound fractions, indicating reduced Cd mobility. Se NPs also enhanced root iron plaque formation and increased the retention of Fe, Cd, and Se on the root surface. XPS analysis showed that 50 and 100 nm Se NPs increased the proportion of Fe(III) in root iron plaque, thereby strengthening Cd adsorption and immobilization at the root–soil interface. In addition, Se NP treatments reshaped rhizosphere microbial communities. The 50 nm treatment showed stronger effects on fungal taxa related to organic matter decomposition and Se activation, whereas the 100 nm treatment more effectively enriched bacterial groups associated with Fe and S cycling, including Thermodesulfobacteriota and Sideroxydans. Among all treatments, 100 nm Se NPs at 20 mg/kg reduced grain Cd from 0.466 to 0.050 mg/kg, representing an 89.27% reduction, while maintaining grain Se at 0.384 mg/kg, which is within the acceptable range for selenium-enriched rice. Overall, 100 nm Se NPs showed the best balance among Cd mitigation, Se biofortification, and ecological safety, suggesting their potential for application in safe rice production in Cd-contaminated paddy soils.

### Role of Symbiotic and Non-Symbiotic Microbes in Legume Rhizosphere

Legume crops are integral to sustainable agriculture due to their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic associations with soil microbes. The rhizosphere, the narrow zone of soil surrounding legume roots, is rich in microbial life that significantly influences plant growth and soil health. This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the functional roles of both symbiotic and non-symbiotic microbes in the legume rhizosphere. Symbiotic microbes, primarily Rhizobium species, form nodules on legume roots and facilitate biological nitrogen fixation (BNF), converting inert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-usable forms. This natural nitrogen input reduces dependency on synthetic fertilisers and enhances soil fertility. In addition, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) establish mutualistic associations with legume roots, improving phosphorus and micronutrient uptake, water absorption, and resistance to environmental stresses. Non-symbiotic microbes, such as plant growth–promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), phosphate-solubilising microorganisms (PSMs), and free-living nitrogen fixers, contribute to nutrient mobilisation, hormone production (e.g. auxins, gibberellins), and stress tolerance without forming specific symbiotic structures. These microbes promote root elongation, increase nutrient-use efficiency, and enhance disease suppression. The chapter also discusses the complex interactions among microbial consortia in the rhizosphere, where competition and cooperation influence microbial colonisation and efficacy. Such interactions are critical in shaping the overall health and productivity of legumes under varying soil and climatic conditions.

### Legume Metagenomics

Legumes are fundamental to global nutritional stability and agricultural sustainability because they provide about 30% of dietary protein and enhance soil health. Still, production faces challenges from environmental stresses, poor nutrients, and diseases. Recently, metagenomics has become a useful tool to study legume rhizosphere, nodules, and inner plant tissues, offering paths to stronger crops. Advances in next-generation sequencing have uncovered a wide variety among these microbial genes that are involved in fixing nitrogen, phosphorus solubilisation, and stress tolerance. Several studies on legume plants under stresses a certain microbial group associated with some cultivars enhances nutrient absorption and boost stress tolerance. A paradigm shift in the crop improvement field may be attained through the integration of metagenomics with genomics-assisted and microbiome-assisted breeding, enabling the selection of plant traits that are complimentary to beneficial microbes. Assessing the active genetic material, functional metagenomics can discover novel genes that code for enzymes, natural antimicrobial compounds, and stress-resilient compounds, making it useful for the development of biofertilisers and biopesticides. Microbial inoculant field results and complex data processing hurdles aside, legume metagenomics shows promise for sustainable agriculture. Challenges remain, and advancements in research will occur. Construction of synthetic microbial communities, improved climate-focused precision agriculture, and multi-omic integration are aiding in the creation of climate-resilient legumes with beneficial microbial partnerships. These legumes will help improve nutrition and health for the world’s population.
