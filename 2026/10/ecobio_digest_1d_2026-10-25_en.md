### Successive planting changes soil physical and chemical properties, enzymatic activities, and microbial communities in Chinese fir(Cunninghamia lanceolata) plantations in China

Chinese fir ( Cunninghamia lanceolata (Lamb.) Hook.) is a major commercial tree species in southern China, where successive planting is widely adopted as the dominant forest management practice. However, repeated planting can disrupt nutrient cycling processes, accelerate soil degradation, and ultimately threaten long-term forest productivity and ecosystem sustainability. Soil physicochemical properties, enzymatic activities, and microbial communities are widely recognized as key indicators of soil health; nevertheless, the effects of successive planting on these attributes in Chinese fir plantations have not been comprehensively synthesized. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a meta-analysis based on 2224 paired observations extracted from 121 independent studies to quantitatively assess the impacts of successive planting relative to first-generation forests. The results demonstrated that successive planting significantly increased soil bulk density, decreased topsoil (0–20 cm) pH, and increased subsoil (>20 cm) pH. In both soil layers, successive planting led to marked reductions in soil organic carbon, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, total potassium, available potassium, and microbial biomass carbon. In addition, ammonium nitrogen declined in the topsoil but increased in the subsoil. Successive planting also substantially altered soil microbial community composition, including shifts in the relative abundance of major microbial phyla, a decline in bacterial diversity, and changes in microbial community indices. These effects were further influenced by multiple environmental and management factors, including planting rotation intensity, stand age, mean annual precipitation, mean annual temperature, altitude, and latitude. Overall, successive planting of Chinese fir exerts detrimental effects on soil pH regulation, carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus pools, as well as bacterial community structure. Our findings highlight the urgent need for targeted management strategies, such as agroforestry rotations and the application of appropriate soil amendments, to alleviate soil degradation and promote the sustainable management of Chinese fir plantation ecosystems.

### Impact of extreme hydrological events on soil microbiome dynamics and ecosystem resilience

Anthropogenic climate change is driving a fundamental destabilization of global climate systems, transitioning historical norms toward a regime characterized by extreme hydrological events, including prolonged droughts and severe flooding. These acute “pulse” disturbances exert severe physiological shocks on the soil microbiome, a critical biological engine responsible for global biogeochemical cycling, greenhouse gas dynamics, and macroscopic ecosystem resilience. Severe drought dismantles microbial communities through intense osmotic stress and restricted substrate diffusion, leading to widespread cellular lysis, significantly reducing microbial biomass, and suppressing the catalytic efficiency of extracellular enzymes. Conversely, extreme flooding plunges the highly heterogeneous soil matrix into rapid anoxia, triggering predictable taxonomic successions, the accumulation of phytotoxins. Furthermore, extreme flooding and subsequent water-table fluctuations drive profound shifts in carbon preservation mechanisms, governed by the “Enzyme Latch” (during persistent anoxia) and the “Iron Gate” (driven by redox changes during subsequent drainage) hypotheses. The violent, rapid transition between these extremes drought abruptly followed by severe rainfall induces the “Birch Effect,” causing a massive, transient pulse of soil respiration that drastically reduces microbial Carbon Use Efficiency (CUE) and accelerates the depletion of ancient carbon pools. To widen ecosystem tolerance thresholds and accelerate recovery from these pulse disturbances, this review evaluates the implementation of Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) interventions, specifically highlighting the synergistic integration of biochar and continuous cover cropping. Rather than completely mitigating extreme physical forces, these targeted land management strategies buffer mild-to-moderate moisture extremes, provide structural refugia to preserve baseline microbial functionality, and align with global sustainability initiatives to secure more climate-resilient terrestrial ecosystems.

### Microbially mediated interactions between enhanced weathering and carbon inputs shift soil organic carbon dynamics

Abstract Enhanced weathering, adding crushed silicate minerals to soils, is being explored as a way to draw down atmospheric carbon dioxide, but its effects on soil organic carbon remain uncertain. We show that olivine amendments change soil carbon cycling depending on time and on labile carbon availability. In a 55-week soil microcosm experiment, adding olivine alone reduces total soil organic carbon. Adding olivine with glucose initially suppresses microbial carbon uptake, but by week 55 instead boosts microbial biomass and mineral-associated organic matter more than glucose alone, while lowering respiratory carbon loss. These late-stage gains grow stronger alongside geochemical signs of mineral weathering. Nanoscale imaging shows that newly formed soil organic carbon accumulates within pre-existing organic and mineral microsites rather than directly on olivine or newly formed clay surfaces. Our findings suggest that when labile carbon is available, enhanced weathering can increase soil carbon storage primarily through microbial processing, rather than by creating new mineral surfaces for carbon to bind to.

### Community differentiation and assembly of soil nosZ-type denitrifiers across dominant plant communities in an alpine wetland of Qinghai Lake

Background Nitrous oxide (N₂O) reduction mediated by nosZ -harboring microorganisms represents the terminal step of denitrification and an important biological sink for N₂O in wetland soils, yet how naturally occurring dominant plant communities are associated with the diversity, taxonomic differentiation, and assembly of nosZ -type communities in alpine wetlands remains poorly understood. Methods We investigated four dominant plant communities in wetlands surrounding Qinghai Lake on the northeastern Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, dominated by Blysmus sinocompressus (BS), Carex regescens (CR), Elymus nutans (EN), and Leymus secalinus (LS), and integrated nosZ -targeted amplicon sequencing with vegetation biomass, soil physicochemical properties, environmental-association analyses, and phylogeny-based null models to evaluate variation in community diversity, taxonomic composition, environmental correlates, and assembly processes. Results The four plant communities exhibited pronounced differences in vegetation–soil conditions and supported distinct nosZ -type communities. LS-associated soils showed the highest richness and the largest pool of community-specific OTUs, whereas Proteobacteria and Bradyrhizobium dominated the overall taxonomic composition. Environmental associations differed between community diversity and taxonomic structure: diversity was more strongly associated with plant biomass, soil temperature, and carbon and nitrogen status, whereas compositional variation was more closely related to soil C ratio, pH, total carbon, moisture, and nitrate nitrogen. Phylogeny-based null-model analysis further indicated that community assembly was predominantly associated with variable selection, although the relative contributions of dispersal limitation and other stochastic processes differed among dominant plant communities. Conclusion These findings demonstrate that dominant plant communities in the Qinghai Lake alpine wetland are associated with coordinated differentiation in vegetation–soil environments, nosZ -type community composition, and assembly processes, thereby characterizing the taxonomic differentiation and inferred assembly of the primer-defined soil nosZ community across contrasting plant-community habitats; however, the implications for realized N₂O reduction require direct validation using gene-abundance, transcriptional, enzyme-activity, and gas-flux measurements.

### Sugarcane–peanut intercropping improves rhizosphere fungal communities and metabolites following long-term continuous peanut monoculture

Background Sugarcane-peanut intercropping improves land use efficiency and crop productivity, yet the rhizosphere mechanisms through which this system alleviates continuous peanut monoculture soil sickness and regulates rhizosphere fungal-metabolite balance remain poorly understood. Methods This study integrated amplicon sequencing and untargeted metabolomics to investigate fungal community dynamics and metabolite profiles in the peanut rhizosphere under monoculture and three intercropping row spacings (2.0 m, 2.4 m, and 3.0 m). Results Intercropping produced coordinated, row-spacing-dependent shifts in peanut rhizosphere fungal taxa, metabolite profiles, land-use efficiency, and kernel quality. Although overall fungal and metabolite beta-diversity differences were not significant, SP2.4 showed the largest set of differentially abundant fungal taxa, while SP2.0 achieved the highest land equivalent ratio. Across spacings, intercropping depleted several Aspergillus and Fusarium taxa and enriched candidate beneficial fungi, including Trichoderma species. Untargeted metabolomics further revealed spacing-specific enrichment of phenolic, stilbenoid, and terpenoid compounds with reported roles in plant defense or fungal inhibition. A feature putatively annotated as aflatoxin B 1 by untargeted LC–MS/MS library matching was less abundant under intercropping. Together, the results indicate that sugarcane–peanut intercropping can improve the rhizosphere microecological state of long-term monocropped peanut fields through coordinated fungal and metabolic reorganization. A row-spacing window of 2.0–2.4 m provided the most favorable balance between system productivity and belowground microecological improvement.

### Associations among root-derived metabolites, the rhizosphere microbiome, nitrogen cycling, and metabolic characteristics of Codonopsis pilosula under biofertilizer–nitrogen coupling

Nitrogen-saving management is essential for improving productivity and understanding metabolic characteristics in cultivated Codonopsis pilosula , yet the stage-dependent coordination of root exudation, rhizosphere microbiome assembly, soil nitrogen cycling, and plant metabolism under biofertilizer-nitrogen coupling remains unclear. Here, C. pilosula was examined at vining, flowering, fruiting, and harvest stages under 10 treatments, including an unfertilized drip-irrigation control and nine combinations of a composite growth-promoting bacterial inoculant with three nitrogen rates. Compared with the unfertilized and uninoculated drip-irrigation control, the intermediate combined treatment showed 16.4%, 26.1%, 36.5%, 51.2%, and 234.7% higher harvest dry weight, fresh weight, root length, root diameter, and soluble protein content, respectively. The intermediate treatment combining an inoculant concentration of OD600 = 0.6 with 120 mg N pot −1 used 33.3% less nitrogen than the corresponding higher-N treatment receiving 180 mg N pot −1 , while showing 17.2% and 36.5% higher dry and fresh weight, respectively. Across treatments, total nitrogen and nitrate nitrogen increased from vining to flowering, and nitrogenase, nitrate reductase, and nitrite reductase activities were enhanced during flowering-fruiting. Rhizosphere bacterial communities showed significant stage-dependent assembly (Adonis R² = 0.112, P = 0.001), and growth stage explained 45.54%, 13.34%, 25.12%, and 37.15% of variation in soil nitrogen cycling, rhizosphere microbiome, root exudates, and plant putative metabolites, respectively. Stage-specific root exudates were associated with nitrogen-cycling bacterial taxa, including Verrucomicrobiota , Gemmatimonadota , Devosia , and Actinobacteriota across vining, flowering, fruiting, and harvest stages. These exudate-associated microbial patterns coincided with nitrogen transformation dynamics and shifts in plant metabolism from early defense to flowering-fruiting anabolism and harvest-stage secondary metabolism. These findings highlight associations between root exudate patterns, stage-specific nitrogen-cycling bacterial communities, and nitrogen-saving cultivation strategies for cultivated medicinal industrial crops.

### Native plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria influence selected soil properties and cocoa seedling development under nursery conditions in Western Boyacá, Colombia

Bioinoculant production requires multidisciplinary expertise to ensure reliable field performance. This study evaluated four native rhizobacterial isolates from the cocoa rhizosphere for inclusion in biofertilizing microbial consortia, assessing effects on soil enzymatic activity, physicochemical properties, and dasometric variables under nursery conditions in Pauna, Boyacá - Colombia. Seven treatments were compared: two native consortia (single-dose and serial application), a commercial inoculant, a chemical fertilizer, and an uninoculated control, using a completely randomized design with five replicate blocks of 20 plants per treatment. Three composite soil samples per treatment were analyzed at 0 and 6 months. Native consortia comprised phosphorus-solubilizing Serratia spp. and plant growth-promoting Pseudomonas spp., while the commercial inoculant contained Azotobacter chroococcum and Pseudomonas fluorescens. Molecular characterization revealed high functional redundancy among the selected strains, with strong potential for phosphate solubilization, colonization, and growth promotion, consistent with overlapping ecological niches. Bioinoculants slightly increased cellulase activity, organic carbon, total nitrogen, and organic matter, whereas chemical fertilization enhanced available phosphorus and exchangeable magnesium while lowering pH. Only boron, pH, phosphorus, and magnesium differed significantly among treatments (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis linked bioinoculants to greater enzymatic and organic matter variability, and fertilizers to higher C/N ratio and phosphorus. Dasometric traits remained unaffected, indicating treatment-specific substrate effects favoring plant growth, pending field-scale validation. Limitations include the nursery-only setting, six-month evaluation period, and lack of field/biosafety validation.

### Green Manure Effects on Soil Microbiota Depend on Seasonal Cropping Cycle, Manure Species, and Kohlrabi Cultivar

ABSTRACT Introduction Green manure application is widely used to improve soil fertility, but its effects on soil microbial communities are context‐dependent. This study examined how green manure species and seasonal cropping cycle influence root‐zone soil microbiomes, soil chemistry, and kohlrabi growth. Materials and Methods A randomised block field experiment tested three green manure species and two kohlrabi cultivars across two seasonal cropping cycles: October sowing and March incorporation followed by kohlrabi cultivation from April to June, and April sowing and June incorporation followed by cultivation from July to September. Bacterial and fungal communities were characterised using 16S rRNA gene and ITS amplicon sequencing and qPCR, alongside soil chemistry and plant‐growth measurements. Results Plant responses were generally modest but more favourable in the second cycle, particularly following Phacelia tanacetifolia . Cropping cycle was the main source of microbial variation, while green manure significantly affected community composition. Bacterial responses differed between cultivars, whereas fungal responses differed between cropping cycles. Treatment effects were driven mainly by compositional turnover rather than increased diversity, with distinct changes in taxon relative abundance. Raphanus sativus and Ph. tanacetifolia altered bacterial composition in cultivar Luna, while all three green manures altered fungal composition in both cycles. Rhizobiales taxa frequently showed higher relative abundance following Pisum sativum , whereas Ph. tanacetifolia was associated with the largest number differentially abundant bacterial taxa. Several detected taxa belonged to groups previously linked to plant–soil processes, although these patterns do not demonstrate microbial function. Soil chemistry differed primarily between cropping cycles and showed only a limited number of associations with microbial composition or diversity and none with qPCR‐based microbial abundance. Conclusions Green manure effects on root‐zone soil microbiomes depend strongly on species, cropping cycle and crop cultivar. Integrating these context‐dependent responses with direct functional analyses may help optimise green manure‐based cropping systems for sustainable agriculture.
