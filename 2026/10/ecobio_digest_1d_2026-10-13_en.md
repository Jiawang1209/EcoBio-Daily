### Persistent soil acidification contrasts with context-dependent enzyme and microbial responses after two decades of nitrogen addition in two subtropical forest types

This dataset contains raw data from a long-term (two-decade) nitrogen (N) addition experiment conducted in mixed pine–broadleaf forests at Dinghushan, southern China. The study examined the effects of chronic N deposition on belowground biological responses across seasons and soil depths. The experiment includes three N addition levels: control (C, 0 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), low nitrogen (L, 50 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), and medium nitrogen (M, 100 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Soil samples were collected from six plots in two soil layers (0–10 cm and 10–20 cm) during both dry and rainy seasons, yielding 72 samples in total. The dataset comprises: Soil physicochemical properties: soil moisture (%), pH (H₂O), NO₃⁻–N (mg/kg), NH₄⁺–N (mg/kg), total nitrogen (TN, %), total carbon (TC, %), total phosphorus (TP, %), and C:N ratio. Extracellular enzyme activities: acid phosphatase (AP), β-glucosidase (BG), β-xylosidase (BX), cellobiohydrolase (CBH), β-galactosidase (GAL), and N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase (NAG). Microbial community composition (PLFA biomarkers): fungi, Actinobacteria, Gram-positive bacteria (G+), Gram-negative bacteria (G−), arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), total bacteria, and the ratios G+:G− and fungi:bacteria (F:B).

### Short‐term effects of Brassicaceae crop residue on soil nitrification and nodulation in the subsequent field pea

Abstract Glucosinolates (GLS) in Brassicaceae crops are known to suppress soil nitrification. Despite widespread inclusion of Brassicaceae species in pulse crop rotations, their effects on soil nitrification and root nodulation of subsequent pulse crops are poorly understood. This controlled greenhouse study examined the effect of Brassicaceae crop residues on soil nitrification and effective root nodulation in field pea ( Pisum sativum L.). Field pea was grown after spring wheat ( Triticum aestivum L.; zero GLS), Argentine canola ( Brassica napus L.; low GLS), and yellow mustard ( Sinapis alba L.; high GLS). Nine treatments were established with field pea grown on below‐ground residues (BGRs) of each preceding crop; combined BGR and above‐ground residues (AGR) from each crop; two chemical amendments, 4‐hydroxy benzyl isothiocyanate (GLS‐hydrolyzed product) and urea; and an unamended control. Soil nitrification was quantified by total soil‐available nitrogen (nitrate‐nitrogen and ammonium‐nitrogen) and ammonia‐oxidizing microbial abundance (ammonia monooxygenase subunit A [ amo A] gene copy concentration) at pre‐seeding, 100% of flowering, and 90% of physiological maturity (harvest) of field pea. Effective nodulation was quantified at flowering and harvest. At flowering, soils with Brassicaceae residues reduced soil nitrate‐nitrogen concentrations by 16.2%, amo A gene copy concentration by 80.2%, and effective root nodulation by 14.6% compared to wheat residues. Yellow mustard residues suppressed nitrification by 18.8%–35.3% and effective root nodulation in field pea by 5.0%–16.3% compared to Argentine canola residues. Overall, Brassicaceae residues transiently suppress nitrification and nodulation; however, suppressive effects diminished as residues decomposed. This study highlights the importance of residue management while underscoring the need for field‐based validation.

### Data for: Afterlife effects of litter traits on rhizosphere multitrophic stability and plant growth–defense strategies

1. Trait-based approaches increasingly recognize that the afterlife effects of plants are crucial to ecosystem functioning. Empirical evidence linking litter trait-driven belowground processes to aboveground plant growth–defense strategies, however, remains limited.2. We used a long-term field experiment involving annual additions of three contrasting litter types (alfalfa, corn, and rape), with plant and soil samples collected during the fourth and fifth years of litter addition. The litter types differed in six measured traits and spanned a gradient from higher to lower expected decomposability along the litter economics spectrum. We examined how the afterlife effects of litter traits were linked to rhizosphere multitrophic associations and plant growth–defense strategies.3. Along the litter economics spectrum from alfalfa through corn to rape, shoot biomass and soluble sugar concentrations declined, whereas leaf jasmonic acid, salicylic acid, and phenolics increased. These responses were primarily associated with lower litter N and higher C:N and lignin:N ratios. Multitrophic diversity increased with decreasing expected litter decomposability, with a parallel increase in the proportion of positive associations during the fifth year of litter addition. Network stability, defined as the ability of the inferred network to maintain cohesion and connectivity under simulated disturbance, was 84–102% higher under corn and rape litter than under alfalfa litter. Greater stability was associated with lower rhizospheric N availability and a shift from an acquisitive, growth-oriented strategy toward a more conservative, defense-oriented strategy.4. Synthesis. Our findings connect the afterlife effects of litter traits with rhizosphere multitrophic stability, N availability, and plant growth–defense strategies, extending trait-based frameworks from decomposition to plant performance. Determining the generality of these relationships across a broader range of plant species and ecosystems represents an important next step in advancing trait-based predictions of plant–soil feedbacks.

### Coarse Woody Debris of Mixed Tree Species Overrides Soil Matrix Constraints on Microbial Richness and Abundance in an Ancient Broad-Leaved Forest

The mechanisms by which coarse woody debris (CWD) affects soil microbial diversity across different spatial scales and forest disturbance regimes remain poorly understood. This study investigated the impact of CWD from multiple tree species on forest floor bacterial and fungal communities under varying landscape-scale conditions, specifically comparing old-growth forests situated on Phaozems and Retisols with mass-windthrow areas located on loamy Retisols and sandy Podzols. We analyzed both chemical compound and metagenomic profiles of 63 CWD-associated soil samples collected from the Kaluzhskie Zaseki Nature Reserve (European part of Russia). High-throughput Illumina sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene and the ITS region was used to profile both bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. Scale-dependent diversity patterns and taxonomic accumulation were assessed using GLM, NMDS, dbRDA, and iNEXT frameworks. Our findings revealed a profound spatial decoupling in forest floor microbial communities, where a localized reduction in alpha-diversity under CWD drove a sharp increase in landscape-scale gamma-diversity. While standard soil physicochemical properties rigidly dictated baseline matrix richness, CWD operated as a powerful micro-environmental hotspot overriding these constraints. Deadwood inputs filtered out dominant matrix-associated taxa, creating highly specialized micro-refugia characterized by high compositional dispersion. This niche selection was particularly pronounced beneath Quercus logs on Phaozems and Retisols. Furthermore, post-windthrow microbial responses were heavily filtered by wood traits and baseline soil textures. On loamy Retisols within the windthrow mosaic, this breakaway dynamic was highly taxon-specific: while Acer and Fraxinus CWD triggered partitioned, site-specific hotspot dynamics yielding an exceptionally large pool of unique ASVs for bacteria, the distinct enrichment for fungal communities was primarily driven by Ulmus CWD and log-free windthrow areas. Ultimately, iNEXT accumulation profiles confirmed that deadwood does not merely multiply common soil taxa, but enriches the forest landscape by injecting critical beta-level heterogeneity into the soil matrix. These insights emphasize the role of diverse CWD retention in buffering forest microbiomes against large-scale environmental disturbances across heterogeneous soil landscapes.

### Soil carbon and inundation regimes jointly regulate denitrification potential in restored riverine floodplains

Denitrification plays a key role in nitrogen removal in restored floodplains, yet the relative importance of inundation regimes, hydrogeomorphic setting, and soil properties remains uncertain. In this study, we evaluated the environmental controls on denitrification potential across four restored sites using a combination of machine learning, structural equation modeling (SEM), and Copula-based analysis. Among the tested models, Random Forest provided the best predictive performance and explained 71% of the variance. Variable importance and partial dependence results identified total carbon (TC), soil moisture content (MC), and bulk density (BD) as the most influential predictors of denitrification potential. Specifically, a threshold in TC between 3% and 5% was associated with a sharp increase in denitrification potential. SEM results indicated that hydrological variables primarily influence denitrification indirectly through their effects on soil moisture and carbon accumulation, accounting for up to 44.4% of the variance at the site level. Copula analysis further quantified the joint probability of elevated denitrification enzyme activity (DEA) under combinations of favorable hydrologic conditions and sufficient carbon availability. Based on these patterns, the sites were grouped into three functional regimes: hydrology-carbon coupled regulation, carbon-limited regulation, and substrate-driven regulation with hydrological enhancement. Together, these results indicate that carbon availability establishes the biogeochemical potential for denitrification, whereas hydrological and physical conditions regulate its expression, with the relative importance of these controls varying among sites. The findings highlight the need for site-specific restoration strategies, including rebuilding soil carbon in degraded agricultural areas and maintaining appropriate inundation regimes while preserving favorable soil conditions in carbon-rich systems to enhance nitrogen removal.
