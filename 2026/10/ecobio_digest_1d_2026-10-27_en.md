### Biochar Mitigates N 2 O and NH 3 Emissions for Organic and Inorganic Fertilizers in an Austrian Field Experiment

ABSTRACT Biochar is a promising soil management strategy to reduce reactive nitrogen (N r ) losses from agricultural soils, and replacing mineral fertilizers with organic amendments (OAs) can improve soil health and enhance nutrient recycling. We conducted a three‐year field experiment with maize ( Zea mays ) in Lower Austria to compare organic waste compost and digestates with mineral nitrogen–phosphorus–potassium (NPK) fertilization (175 kg N ha −1 , 39 kg P ha −1 , 187 kg K ha −1 ) and to evaluate the effects of biochar application on nitrous oxide (N 2 O), ammonia (NH 3 ) emissions, and crop yield. Compost and digestates were applied at higher total N rates (350 kg N ha −1 ). Biochar (approximately 7.5 t ha −1 ) was applied in combination with NPK or compost at the same fertilizer application rates as the respective non‐biochar treatments to assess its potential to mitigate gaseous N r losses. Treatment effects on soil properties and microbial functional gene abundances were also evaluated. Overall, compost reduced cumulative N 2 O and NH 3 emissions by 66% and 68%, respectively, whereas digestate increased emissions by 21% (N 2 O) and 32% (NH 3 ) compared to NPK. Biochar‐amended soils mitigated N 2 O emissions by 48% under NPK and 39% under compost. Moreover, NH 3 volatilization was reduced by 51% for NPK and 26% for compost when biochar was added. In reference to yield, digestates successfully substituted NPK, whereas compost did not provide equivalent short‐term nutrient supply due to limited N‐mineralization rates. Soil organic carbon and total nitrogen (TN) increased under OAs, indicating long‐term fertility benefits. Reduced N 2 O emissions under biochar amendment could be linked to a lower denitrification potential in these treatments, while the availability of nitrate () and ammonium () was not affected by biochar amendment, as confirmed by analysis of abundance of N cycling genes and soil extractions. Our research demonstrates that both OAs and biochar can serve as climate‐smart agriculture tools by improving soil fertility and supporting C sequestration while reducing direct N 2 O and NH 3 emissions. However, effective fertilization management is necessary to maintain high yields, requiring integrated nutrient management strategies that strategically combine OAs with mineral N fertilization.

### Phenotypic and molecular characterization of microsymbionts associated with Tephrosia strigosa in the central Aravalli region

Legume–rhizobia symbiosis is central to sustainable agriculture due to its role in biological nitrogen fixation. However, rhizobial diversity in ecologically significant regions such as the Aravalli range remains insufficiently explored. This study investigates the microsymbiont diversity associated with Tephrosia strigosa , a legume species for which no prior symbiotic data are available from this region. A total of 83 root nodule bacterial (RNB) strains were isolated from multiple sites across the Central Aravalli range and assessed for their phenotypic and genetic diversity. Based on BOX-PCR fingerprinting, 30 isolates were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. Analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity revealed that 23 RNB isolates were related to rhizobial genera ( Sinorhirobium and Rhizobium ), while 7 belonged to non-rhizobial strains. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that 15 isolates clustered within the genus Sinorhizobium and were closely related to Sinorhizobium aridi , S. mexicanus , S. kostiense , S. adhaerens and S. terangae . Eight isolates clustered with Rhizobium species, including Rhizobium pongamiae , R. bangladeshense , R. pakistanense and R. oryzihabitans , however, among these two isolates (TS3 and TS16) closely related to R. oryzihabitans failed to nodulate their original host in the host authentication test, indicating their non-symbiotic nature. Overall, the phylogenetic structure of RNB isolates associated with T. strigosa demonstrates that nodulation is primarily mediated by Sinorhizobium and Rhizobium species, with a clear dominance of Sinorhizobium . This dominance suggests that Sinorhizobium spp. serve as the primary microsymbionts of native legumes in the Central Aravalli region. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of rhizobial diversity, biogeography, and host specificity in an ecologically important yet understudied ecosystem.

### Metagenomic evidence for climate-associated variation in microbial methylmercury production across peatlands

Abstract Peatlands are major global mercury (Hg) reservoirs and are highly sensitive to warming, yet climate effects on microbial controls of neurotoxic methylmercury (MeHg) formation remain unclear. Here, we combined global synthesis with new measurements to assess climate-associated variation in net MeHg production. Metagenomes from 144 peat soils revealed higher relative abundance of the Hg-methylation gene hgcA but lower relative abundance of the demethylation gene merB at high latitudes, consistent with their opposing correlations with mean annual temperature. The ratio of nitrogen-fixation to denitrification genes ( nifH / nosZ ) covaried with hgcA and merB , suggesting links between nitrogen cycling and Hg transformation. Genome-resolved analyses further showed expanded denitrification pathways in hgcA -bearing taxa from warmer peatlands. Together, these findings indicate that warmer climates may be associated with microbial community restructuring and lower net MeHg production. A space-for-time framework therefore suggests reduced MeHg yields under future warming, informing Hg-risk assessment under the Minamata Convention.

### A life-history axis of resource acquisition in bacteria: depolymerisation versus uptake

Microbial decomposition of complex organic matter is a major control on the global carbon cycle, and its efficiency hinges on how microbes acquire resources. Decomposition requires costly extracellular enzymes to break down complex polymers, whereas the resulting soluble products are captured far more cheaply through membrane transporters. How microbes balance investment between these two processes is a fundamental axis of microbial life history, yet it has remained difficult to quantify across their vast diversity. Using genomic repertoires for carbohydrate depolymerisation and uptake, we developed a simple ratio distinguishing depolymerisation-optimised from uptake-optimised strategies, and applied it to more than 249,000 environmental and host-associated bacterial genomes. These strategies were strongly phylogenetically structured and differed by habitat, with uptake-optimised bacteria unexpectedly more prevalent in diffusion-limited soils than in aquatic environments. In an 18-month field-scale plant litter decomposition experiment, uptake-optimised bacteria dominated during early decomposition, when fungi were more abundant and strongly expressed hydrolytic enzymes, shifting towards depolymerisation-optimised bacteria at later stages. A simple genomic ratio therefore captures ecologically meaningful resource-acquisition strategies that vary with phylogeny, habitat and successional stage. The balance of these strategies could shape community-level extracellular enzyme investment, with consequences for decomposition and carbon cycling.

### Natural farming as a pathway to climate-resilient soils

Soil health underpins agricultural productivity, nutrient cycling, water regulation and ecosystem functioning, yet intensive agricultural management can adversely affect several physical, chemical and biological soil properties when inputs and management practices are inappropriate or excessive. Natural farming (NF) has emerged as a low-external-input agroecological approach that emphasizes locally available farm resources, biological processes and practices such as Beejamrit, Jeevamrit, mulching and crop diversification. This review critically synthesizes current evidence on the effects of NF and its component practices on soil physical, chemical and biological properties and examines the mechanisms through which these changes may influence soil functions relevant to climate resilience. Available studies report improvements in selected indicators, including soil organic carbon, microbial activity, nutrient availability, soil aggregation and water-related properties under NF or associated bio-organic management practices. However, the magnitude and consistency of these responses vary with soil type, crop, climate, duration of management and the combination of inputs used, and some reported benefits cannot be attributed exclusively to NF because organic manures, microbial inoculants or reduced mineral-fertilizer inputs were applied concurrently. Potential improvements in soil organic matter, aggregation, water retention, nutrient cycling and biological activity may contribute to greater buffering against drought, heat and rainfall variability; nevertheless, direct long-term evidence demonstrating enhanced climate resilience under NF remains limited. Important research priorities include long-term and multi-location comparisons, nutrient budgeting, soil carbon stock assessment, greenhouse-gas measurements, standardization of NF formulations and evaluation of yield and economic trade-offs. Overall, current evidence indicates that NF has potential to support soil functions relevant to climate resilience, but the magnitude and persistence of these benefits require further rigorous, context-specific evaluation.

### Soil Biogeochemical Cycles and Their Influence on Crop-Environment Dynamics in Changing Climates

Soil biogeochemical cycles regulate nutrient availability, soil organic matter dynamics, greenhouse-gas exchange, and crop productivity, but their functioning is increasingly modified by climate change. This review synthesizes the major processes controlling carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), and silicon (Si) cycling in agricultural soils and examines how these interconnected cycles influence soil-crop-environment relationships under changing climatic conditions. Warming, drought, excessive soil moisture, altered precipitation, and elevated atmospheric CO2 can modify microbial activity, organic-matter turnover, nutrient mineralization and immobilization, redox conditions, nutrient losses, and greenhouse-gas emissions. Their effects are strongly context dependent and vary with soil texture, pH, organic-matter content, drainage, microbial communities, crop type, and management. The review further evaluates how plant nutrient acquisition, rhizosphere processes, and plant-soil-microbial feedbacks connect biogeochemical cycling with crop performance and environmental outcomes. Conservation agriculture, organic amendments, biochar, and microbial inoculants may improve nutrient retention and soil resilience, but their effectiveness depends on soil, climate, amendment properties, and management history and may involve agronomic or environmental trade-offs. We identify major knowledge gaps concerning interactions among elemental cycles, responses to compound climate extremes, microbial regulation of nutrient transformations, and the transferability of management practices across agroecosystems. Integrating long-term field experiments, process-based modelling, molecular approaches, and high-resolution soil monitoring will be essential for developing climate-resilient nutrient-management strategies.

### Building Bridges Belowground: Pythium oligandrum En-hances Rhizosphere Network Connectivity

Biological control agents (BCAs) offer a potential sustainable alternative to fungicides for crop dis-ease management, but their effects on resident plant microbiomes and microbial interactions remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated the effects of the BCA Pythium oligandrum on disease con-trol and the rhizosphere microbiome of starch potato cv. Kuras in a field trial. We compared a labora-tory strain and a commercial formulation (Polygandron) with commonly used fungicides during early blight development. The laboratory strain suppressed early blight at plant senescence, but this effect did not persist until harvest, whereas fungicide treatment provided near-complete disease suppression throughout the growing season. P. oligandrum caused transient changes in rhizosphere fungal com-munity composition at flowering, while bacterial community richness varied among sampling times in untreated controls. However, bacterial co-occurrence network analysis revealed pronounced treat-ment-specific changes in microbial connectivity. Application of the laboratory strain increased net-work connectivity, resulting in almost a threefold increase in mean degree and greater interconnected-ness among bacterial taxa. In contrast, Polygandron and fungicide treatments reduced mean degree, indicating reduced connectivity within the bacterial network. Fungicide treatment additionally altered network organisation, including increased betweenness centrality and changes in modularity. Togeth-er, our results show that P. oligandrum can modify microbial interactions in the potato rhizosphere without substantially altering overall bacterial community composition, highlighting the value of in-tegrating microbial network structure with community-level analyses when evaluating the ecological effects of BCAs.
