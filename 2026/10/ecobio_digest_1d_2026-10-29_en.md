### Saline versus alkaline stresses in paddy soils drive divergent microbial nutrient limitations despite shared carbon constraints

Abstract Soil salinization and alkalization often suppress microbial functional activity, triggering negative feedback loops in microbial metabolism, degrading nutrients and agricultural productivity. However, the impacts of saline versus alkaline soils on microbial metabolism remain unclear. We compared reclaimed coastal saline ( S 1 ) and Songnen alkaline ( A 1 ) paddy soils with their corresponding non‐saline controls ( S 0 and A 0 ) using models of ecoenzymatic stoichiometry, aggregate fractionation, amplicon sequencing and high‐throughput qPCR to quantify microbial carbon (C), nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) limitation and their abiotic and biotic drivers. Our findings revealed that saline and alkaline stresses consistently intensified microbial C limitation by 24.79% and 90.43%, respectively, relative to their corresponding CK soils. However, the two regional soil systems showed divergent nutrient limitation patterns associated with contrasting physicochemical and microbial conditions. Saline soils exhibited acute microbial N limitation (VT N/P limitation = −6.51), which was associated with lower N availability, including 53.29% lower DTN than in alkaline soils, macroaggregate‐associated N depletion, and bacterial‐associated network reorganization. Microbial functional profiles further indicated greater genetic potential for denitrification and lower genetic potential for dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium, with A 1 soils having 25.12% lower nirK/S abundance and 49.68% higher napA abundance than S 1 soils. In contrast, alkaline soils exhibited stronger microbial P limitation (VT N/P limitation = 9.73), which was associated with reduced P bioavailability, including 56.68% lower AP than in saline soils, greater P sequestration in silt+clay fractions, and fungal‐associated network reorganization. This pattern was accompanied by stronger suppression of genes associated with organic P mineralization and inorganic P solubilization, with phnK and gcd + pqqC abundances being 28.27% and 25.19% lower than in S 1 soils, respectively. These results show that divergent microbial nutrient limitations arise from coupled soil physicochemical constraints, microbial community reassembly, network reorganization and functional gene responses, rather than from ion‐specific chemistry alone. These results highlight organic matter restoration as a common priority, but indicate divergent N/P fertilization strategies for saline and alkaline soils. These insights provide a fundamental framework for targeted remediation of saline and alkaline soils and facilitate sustainable agricultural practices by integrating both universal and salt‐type‐specific methodological approaches. ### Combination of cover crops and microbial biostimulants: Impacts on soil functioning, grape production, and juice quality in rainfed vineyards

Vineyards are intensively managed agroecosystems, which can reduce their soil functioning and biodiversity, with consequences for grape and wine quality. New regulations (e.g., Europe’s green policies) and market demand for environmentally respectful products are encouraging the adoption of a range of nature-based practices such as cover crops and microbial biostimulants, yet their combined effects remain poorly evaluated. We conducted a fully factorial field experiment in rainfed vineyards in southern Spain to assess the simultaneous effects of two sustainable management strategies: the use of winter cover crops, as opposed to year-round tillage, and the foliar application of a nitrogen-fixing microbial biostimulant at two doses (1x and 3x based on the recommendations of the manufacturer). Cover crops increased the activity of soil extracellular enzymes associated with the C, N and P cycles, indicating enhanced decomposition potential, whereas the biostimulant did not consistently alter soil enzyme activity. Across two seasons, cover crops slightly reduced yield, but this effect was non-significant, while the biostimulant increased production in the first vintage. Importantly, the combination of cover crops with a high biostimulant dose achieved yields comparable to, or even higher than, tilled controls. The combined treatments suggested potential synergistic responses in ºBrix (indicative of total soluble solids) and pH of musts. Using structural equation modelling, we provided additional mechanistic evidence that cover crops and microbial biostimulants are two compatible strategies that may enhance, through complementary mechanisms (i.e., promoting either soil or plant health), the sustainability of vineyards. Our findings support the combination of these practices as a viable pathway toward more resilient and environmentally respectful viticulture.

### Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization by five isolates of Glomeromycotan fungi after storage along a year at two contrasting temperatures with Vigna luteola as test plant.

Due to the important functions of arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM), they are considered crucial for both agriculture and reforestation. Although mycorrhizal inoculant production has grown significantly, information about the effect of time and storage conditions on its performance is very scarce. The aim of this study is to assess the infectivity of five different AMF inocula, stored along a year at two contrasting temperatures (2 and 20 °C). Plant growth, percentage of mycorrhizal colonization (AM%), arbuscules (Arb%) and vesicles (Ves%) were assessed monthly, using Vigna luteola as test plant. Acaulospora lacunosa, Funneliformis mosseae, and Gigaspora gigantea presented a better performance when they were stored at 20 °C than at 2°C, while Rhizoglomus manihotis and Dentiscutata heterogama were not affected by the temperature of storage. Inoculants had a positive effect on plant growth throughout the year, reaching a maximum value after 4 months of storage. AM% and Arb% decreased throughout the year of storage, whereas Ves% remained high in R. manihotis (both temperatures) and A. lacunosa (20°C). These findings are discussed in light of the biology of the evaluated AMF species. The inoculants produced must be stored at room temperature for greater preservation of their infective capacity.

### Diversified crop rotation reshapes soil nutrients, microbial communities, and functions in sorghum fields

Introduction Long-term continuous sorghum cropping degrades soil environments and causes continuous cropping obstacles. This study investigated whether diversified crop rotation can maintain soil health and improve the soil microenvironment for sorghum growth. Methods Using continuous sorghum cropping as the control (SSS), three rotation systems were established: sorghum-potato-sorghum (SPS), sorghum-common vetch-sorghum (SVS), and sorghum-green pea shoots-sorghum (SGS). Soil physicochemical properties, microbial community structure and diversity were analyzed using Illumina NovaSeq high-throughput sequencing, and functional traits were predicted using FAPROTAX and FUNGuild. Mantel tests and co-occurrence network analysis were also performed. Results All three rotation treatments improved soil physicochemical properties to varying degrees, with SPS showing the greatest nutrient enhancement. SVS markedly increased bacterial richness and diversity, while SPS significantly enhanced fungal richness and diversity. β-diversity analysis revealed that rotation treatments differed significantly from SSS, with SVS and SGS showing similar bacterial structures and SPS and SVS showing similar fungal structures. Functionally important microbial groups increased in rotation soils, whereas the pathogenic genus Fusarium remained relatively high in SPS. Mantel tests indicated that soil pH, TN, TP, TK, and AK were key factors influencing microbial community structure, with AK showing the strongest correlation. Nitrogen fixation and cellulose degradation were the dominant bacterial functions, while saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungi were enriched in rotation soils. Co-occurrence network analysis showed that the rotation network had higher average degree, network density, modularity, and a larger connected component than the continuous cropping network. Discussion These findings indicate that diversified crop rotation effectively elevates soil nutrient levels, reshapes microbial community structure and ecological functions, and supports high-quality and stable sorghum production.

### Host identity and fungal source pools shape mycorrhizal assembly under carbon removal interventions in large-scale afforestation

1. Large-scale afforestation is central to global carbon dioxide removal (CDR) commitments. Soil inoculation and enhanced rock weathering (ERW) may increase tree biomass carbon stocks during woodland establishment. Both can alter soil fungal communities, but whether they change the root-colonising mycorrhizal fungi that support tree nutrition and growth is largely unknown. 2. In a fully-factorial field trial on former upland pasture in Wales (11.5 ha; ~25,000 saplings), we tested the effects of soil inoculation (addition of soil from nearby mature, donor forests) and ERW (basalt amendment) on root-associated ectomycorrhizal (EcM) and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, and tree growth in mixed-broadleaf and conifer plantations, two and four years after planting. To identify key colonisation sources, we also characterised mycorrhizal fungi in roots and soils of surrounding hedgerows, source tree nursery, non-forested control plots, donor woodlands and soil inoculum. 3. Soil inoculation caused a subtle shift in conifer EcM community composition (3% of variation) but had no detectable effect in broadleaves. Basalt amendment raised soil pH but did not change mycorrhizal composition, diversity, or colonisation. 4. Root-associated EcM communities were distinct from those detected in plantation soils and shared far more taxa with the tree nursery than with the donor inoculum (41-48% vs. 2-9% of plantation EcM taxa) whereas AM communities largely reflected fungi present in pre-existing pasture. 5. Host identity was the dominant factor structuring mycorrhizal communities, overshadowing both treatments, and was the strongest predictor of aboveground biomass. 6. Synthesis and applications: This field-scale evaluation shows early mycorrhizal assembly during woodland establishment is strongly structured by tree species identity and pre-existing fungal source pools, limiting the effect of inoculation at planting. Mycorrhizal management should therefore target the nursery stage and be tailored to individual tree species. Basalt amendment for ERW is unlikely to harm mycorrhizal communities during establishment. Because soil fungal communities did not reflect root symbiosis, our findings also stress that monitoring of belowground outcomes in CDR-focused planting should include root sampling rather than relying on soil DNA alone.
