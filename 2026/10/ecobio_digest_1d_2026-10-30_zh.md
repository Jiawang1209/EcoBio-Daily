### Burn intensity regulates post-fire effects on soil N2O emissions: A grassland soil pyrocosm study

Post-fire soil emissions of nitric oxide (NO) and nitrous oxide (N2O) are important ecosystem nitrogen (N) loss pathways, but their flux dynamics vary widely across studies, making generalizations challenging. Natural variation in burn intensity--or heat energy released during combustionm--may help resolve this variability by mediating the soil physicochemical and biological drivers of these emissions. Here, we used an experimental "pyrocosm" system to evaluate how varying burn intensity influences post-fire NO and N2O emissions using soil from a Southern California grassland across 18 weeks. Over several months, we measured soil NO and N2O fluxes alongside soil pH, extractable nitrate (NO3-) and ammonium (NH4+), net NH4+ production, net nitrification, soil carbon (C) pools, microbial biomass C, and the potential activities of five extracellular hydrolytic enzymes under Control, Low- (100.1 {+/-} 20.0 oC peak soil temperature at 5 cm depth), and High-intensity (243.8 {+/-} 7.4 oC peak soil temperature at 5 cm depth) burns. Soil NO emissions were not significantly affected by burn intensity. In contrast, N2O emissions tripled under low burn intensity relative to unburned controls, but did not differ from controls at high burn intensity. This stimulation of N2O emission under low burn intensity was driven by increased denitrification, as supported by elevated soil pH and labile C availability, and an 88% reduction in net nitrification that signaled active NO3- consumption. Under high burn intensity, however, severe loss of microbial biomass, soil C, and NO3- constrained denitrification, with rising hydrolytic enzyme activities over time suggesting C limitation. Together, these findings demonstrate that post-fire N2O emissions reflect a fire intensity-dependent balance between microbial mortality and resource limitation, helping to explain divergent N emissions across ecosystems.

### Integrated soil–microbe–plant regulation for maize disease control in the black soil of Northeast China

Abstract The black soil region of Northeast China is a critical area for maize production in China. However, the long‐term continuous cropping, coupled with residue‐driven pathogen carryover, soil organic matter depletion, nutrient imbalance, and climate variability, has increased maize vulnerability to diseases. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the integrated regulation of soil, microbiomes, and plant immunity for maize disease management in this region. It assesses the physicochemical properties of the soil, the microbiota, and plant immune responses. Particular attention is paid to current biological control approaches. These plant immunity‐ and microbiome‐based strategies include beneficial microbe‐mediated immune priming, activation of induced systemic resistance, modulation of plant hormone signaling networks, RNA interference (RNAi)‐based immune regulation, and microbiome‐assisted enhancement of disease resistance. The underlying mechanisms include recruitment of beneficial microorganisms, suppression of pathogen growth and virulence, stabilization of protective microbial networks, activation of plant immune signaling, and enhancement of stress resilience. This review also compares these strategies in terms of disease‐control efficacy, yield benefits, stability, cost, biosafety, and commercialization potential, and proposes a region‐specific pathway for their integrated deployment. Establishment of a coordinated regulatory framework that combines soil‐health improvement, microbiome intervention, pathogen monitoring, and plant immune enhancement will support the transition from chemical‐dependent disease control to proactive and sustainable maize production systems in the black soil region of Northeast China.

### Tobacco–barley rotation reshapes soil microbial communities and enriches culturable bacteria with plant growth-promoting potential

Introduction Crop rotation is an important agronomic practice for alleviating the adverse effects of continuous tobacco cropping, yet the effects of preceding gramineous crops on the microbial communities and potential functions of tobacco-growing soils have not been systematically verified. Methods Based on a three-year continuous field experiment, this study used a flue-cured tobacco–fallow system (CK) as the control and established a flue-cured tobacco–barley rotation system (DM). Soil chemical properties and enzyme activities, bacterial 16S rRNA and fungal ITS amplicon sequencing, culturable bacterial isolation, and greenhouse pot reinoculation experiments were integrated to evaluate changes in the soil environment and microbial communities following barley rotation and to verify the plant growth-promoting potential of candidate enriched isolates. Results Compared with CK, DM significantly ( P < 0.05) increased soil dissolved organic carbon, available phosphorus, available potassium, and hydrolyzable nitrogen by 19.65%, 3.23%, 5.45%, and 5.90%, respectively; sucrase, β-glucosidase, acid phosphatase, and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase activities increased by 23.80%, 66.28%, 15.23%, and 176.32%, respectively. Amplicon sequencing showed that barley rotation significantly altered soil bacterial and fungal community. Sphingomonas and Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia were relatively enriched under DM and were significantly associated with multiple soil nutrient and enzyme activity variables; therefore, they were defined as candidate enriched bacterial taxa. Six culturable isolates belonging to these taxa were subsequently obtained from DM soil. In vitro , these isolates exhibited different combinations of functional phenotypes, including growth on nitrogen-free medium, phosphorus transformation, potassium solubilization, zinc solubilization, siderophore production, and organic-substrate degradation. Greenhouse pot experiments showed that mixed inoculation significantly promoted flue-cured tobacco growth and increased plant nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium accumulation. Conclusion Barley rotation was accompanied by significant changes in soil available nutrients, enzyme activities, and microbial community composition, whereas culturable isolates obtained from rotation-enriched taxa showed plant growth-promoting potential under controlled conditions. These findings support the possibility that rotation-enriched microorganisms participate in subsequent plant–soil nutrient feedbacks, although their direct mediating role in field rotation effects requires further verification. This study provides a theoretical basis and candidate microbial resources for understanding the microbial ecological effects of barley–tobacco rotation and screening potential plant growth-promoting bacteria.

### Biofertilizers in Sustainable Crop Production: Linking Rhizosphere Mechanisms, Formulation, and Context-Dependent Field Performance

Biofertilizers are increasingly considered components of resource-efficient nutrient management, but their agronomic performance remains strongly dependent on crop genotype, soil properties, microbial strain, formulation, and management context. This structured critical narrative review evaluates molecular, agronomic, environmental, and implementation evidence on plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, nitrogen-fixing bacteria, phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms, and emerging nano-enabled microbial formulations. Molecular and omics studies have clarified mechanisms of nutrient cycling, colonization, plant signalling, and rhizosphere restructuring, but these indicators alone do not predict field efficacy. A global meta-analysis reported an average yield increase of 16.2% relative to non-inoculated controls, but responses vary across crops and environments, and reliable mineral-fertilizer substitution remains context-dependent. The evidence favors site-specific deployment within integrated nutrient-management systems and assessment based on yield, nutrient-use efficiency, crop quality, resource-use efficiency, and resilience. Waste-derived substrates, biodegradable carriers, and nano-enabled formulations may improve resource circularity and delivery, but their environmental advantages require life-cycle, stability, safety, and efficacy-based evaluation. Future progress depends on multi-environment validation, robust formulations, predictive multi-omics linked to agronomic outcomes, quality standards, and proportionate regulation.

### Rhizosphere Soil Properties and Microbial Community Across an Apple Replanting Chronosequence

Apple replant disease originates from rhizosphere microbial dysbiosis, allelochemical accumulation, and soil physicochemical degradation, causing seedling stunting and obstructing aged orchard renewal. Here, rhizosphere soils were sampled from newly planted control (CK) and 2–7-year replanted apple trees (Y2–Y7) arranged in a cross-sectional space-for-time chronosequence. We characterized soil physicochemical traits and profiled bacterial (16S rDNA) and fungal (ITS) communities via relative abundance amplicon sequencing. Soils from Y2–Y7 had significantly lower concentrations of alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), total nitrogen (TN), total potassium (TK), available zinc (AZn), and available copper (ACu) than CK, but higher exchangeable magnesium (Ex-Mg). Sequencing obtained 5883 bacterial and 2063 fungal ASVs. Y2–Y7 soils showed elevated relative abundances of Bacillota and Pseudomonadota; Ascomycota dominated all samples. Bacterial alpha diversity did not differ significantly among treatments, whereas bacterial beta diversity significantly separated long-term replanted (Y5–Y7) soil from CK. Soil nutrients were associated with bacterial community structure; partial Mantel tests for fungi yielded only trend-level associations, consistent with the non-significant overall fungal PERMANOVA (p = 0.08). This study examines the associations among rhizosphere soil properties and microbial composition across a 2–7-year apple replanting chronosequence and provides correlative evidence linking microbial compositional changes to soil nutrient availability, establishing a baseline for understanding rhizosphere microbiome-soil interactions in replanted orchards.

### Mycorrhizal Networks

Introduction and Definition: Mycorrhizae are widespread symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots that extend fungal access into the surrounding soil. The term mycorrhiza describes this close relationship: “A mycorrhiza is a symbiotic association between a fungus and the roots of a vascular plant.” When mycorrhizal fungal mycelium associates with the roots of multiple plants, it can form a common mycorrhizal network (CMN). Mycorrhizal associations play fundamental roles in terrestrial ecosystems by contributing to nutrient cycling, soil stability, plant growth, and ecosystem resilience. Mycorrhizal symbioses are estimated to have originated approximately 400–450 million years ago, contributing to the colonization of land by early plants.Body: Major forms of mycorrhizal associations include arbuscular, ectomycorrhizal, ericoid, and orchid mycorrhizae, which differ in their fungal partners, structures, and host ranges. In these associations, plants provide photosynthetically derived carbon to fungi, while fungi enhance plant access to water and mineral nutrients. Mycorrhizal associations influence ecosystem functioning by enhancing nutrient acquisition, supporting soil aggregation, and affecting plant community composition and soil carbon cycling. Common mycorrhizal networks have been investigated as potential pathways for the movement of resources and signals among associated plants. Experimental studies have detected movement of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus among plants associated with common fungal mycelia, as well as defence-associated responses in connected plants. However, the magnitude, mechanisms, and ecological consequences of these processes vary among experimental systems and remain under investigation. Knowledge of mycorrhizal symbioses is informing research in sustainable agriculture, forestry, reforestation, and ecosystem restoration. Recent scientific debate has emphasized the importance of distinguishing evidence that plants are connected, evidence that resources or signals move between them, and evidence that these processes provide ecologically meaningful benefits to recipient plants. This distinction is central to ongoing research into the ecological significance of common mycorrhizal networks.

### Synthesis of greenhouse gas emission factors for forest organic soils in the Köppen–Geiger Dfb climate zone

Abstract. Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from organic soils are a key component of land-use-related emissions, particularly in countries with large areas of organic soils. Temperate-zone forest soils remain less studied in GHG research than boreal soils. However, recent work has expanded coverage in the northeastern temperate region, which, under the Köppen–Geiger climate classification, shares key climatic characteristics with the southern boreal region (Dfb). This study synthesised updated GHG flux data to evaluate carbon balance and emissions from forest organic soils in the Dfb zone, stratified by drainage status, nutrient availability, and dominant tree species. Such stratification revealed CO2 source-sink patterns, which encourage the ecological relevance of using these categories for data aggregation. The dominant tree species reflected nutrient status: drained coniferous and deciduous stands have been reported as CO2 sources, emitting 0.03 ± 0.55 and 0.47 ± 0.29 t CO2‑C ha−1 year−1, respectively, though soils tended to shift toward CO2 sinks in stands older than 25 years. In contrast, undrained soils have generally been observed to function as CO2 sinks, although not necessarily in all sites. However, this stratification was less informative for CH4 and N2O. CH4 fluxes were primarily determined by water table level rather than by other site variables, whereas N2O showed a tendency toward elevated emissions in deciduous stands, irrespective of drainage status.
