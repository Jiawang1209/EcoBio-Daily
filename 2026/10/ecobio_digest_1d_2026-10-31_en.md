### Burn intensity regulates post-fire effects on soil N 2 O emissions: A grassland soil pyrocosm study

Post-fire soil emissions of nitric oxide (NO) and nitrous oxide (N 2 O) are important ecosystem nitrogen (N) loss pathways, but their flux dynamics vary widely across studies, making generalizations challenging. Natural variation in burn intensity—or heat energy released during combustionm—may help resolve this variability by mediating the soil physicochemical and biological drivers of these emissions. Here, we used an experimental "pyrocosm" system to evaluate how varying burn intensity influences post-fire NO and N 2 O emissions using soil from a Southern California grassland across 18 weeks. Over several months, we measured soil NO and N 2 O fluxes alongside soil pH, extractable nitrate (NO 3 - ) and ammonium (NH 4 +), net NH 4 + production, net nitrification, soil carbon (C) pools, microbial biomass C, and the potential activities of five extracellular hydrolytic enzymes under Control, Low- (100.1 ± 20.0 o C peak soil temperature at 5 cm depth), and High-intensity (243.8 ± 7.4 o C peak soil temperature at 5 cm depth) burns. Soil NO emissions were not significantly affected by burn intensity. In contrast, N 2 O emissions tripled under low burn intensity relative to unburned controls, but did not differ from controls at high burn intensity. This stimulation of N 2 O emission under low burn intensity was driven by increased denitrification, as supported by elevated soil pH and labile C availability, and an 88% reduction in net nitrification that signaled active NO 3 - consumption. Under high burn intensity, however, severe loss of microbial biomass, soil C, and NO 3 - constrained denitrification, with rising hydrolytic enzyme activities over time suggesting C limitation. Together, these findings demonstrate that post-fire N 2 O emissions reflect a fire intensity-dependent balance between microbial mortality and resource limitation, helping to explain divergent N emissions across ecosystems.

### Structure-activity relationship study in soil identifies an effective broad-spectrum inhibitor of archaeal nitrification

Ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA) can contribute substantially to nitrification in agricultural soils, yet the portfolio of AOA-targeting nitrification inhibitors (NIs) with demonstrated efficacy under soil conditions remains limited. Moreover, the performance of candidate inhibitors in soil is often difficult to predict from pure-culture assays because their activity is strongly influenced by soil-dependent factors. In this study, we employed a recently developed high-throughput miniaturized soil incubation assay to perform a soil-based structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis of SIAS (Specific Inhibitor of AOA in Soil), a pyrazolo-pyridine derivative previously identified as an AOA-targeting compound with strong additive nitrification inhibition effects when combined with the AOB-targeting inhibitor DMP (3,4-dimethyl-1H-pyrazole). To identify structural features associated with improved and more robust soil efficacy, a library of 27 SIAS-derived pyrazolo-pyridine analogues was evaluated directly in soil. The SAR analysis showed that inhibitory activity was governed by a specific combination of structural features and led to the discovery of Optimized SIAS (Opt. SIAS), a simplified analogue that consistently outperformed the original lead compound across diverse agricultural soils. Correlation analysis further revealed that soil pH and, particularly, silt content were major determinants of Opt. SIAS efficacy. The strong additive effects obtained through combined application of Opt. SIAS and DMP highlight the contribution of AOA to residual nitrification following AOB inhibition and demonstrate the potential of simultaneously targeting both groups. Together, these results expand the limited portfolio of effective AOA-targeting nitrification inhibitors and provide a foundation for the development of next-generation nitrification inhibition strategies aimed at improving nitrogen-use efficiency and reducing nitrogen losses from agricultural soils.

### Bacillus subtilis CECT30572 for biological fertilizer stabilization: an alternative to synthetic nitrification inhibitors

Introduction Modern intensive agriculture faces the critical challenge of reduced nitrogen use efficiency and severe environmental impacts associated with nitrate (NO 3 − ) leaching. Although chemical nitrification inhibitors such as 3,4-dimethylpyrazole phosphate (DMPP) are widely used to reduce nitrogen losses, there is growing interest in sustainable, bio-based alternatives. This study investigates Bacillus subtilis CECT30572, the active biological component of the commercial nitrogen stabilizer NSAFE ® , as a microbial strategy to reduce nitrate leaching. Methods Hybrid whole-genome sequencing was performed to characterize genes associated with nitrogen metabolism. In vitro assays evaluated nitrate depletion, and soil column leaching experiments were conducted under contrasting soil types using different fertilizer formulations. Results Hybrid whole-genome sequencing revealed a conserved nasABCDEF cluster and regulatory elements ( tnrA, glnR, codY, gltC ) associated with nitrogen assimilation. The genome also encodes the respiratory narGHJI operon for nitrate reduction to nitrite (NO 2 > − ), while lacking canonical genes for complete denitrification ( nirK/S, norBC, nosZ ), indicating a non-denitrifying nitrogen metabolism. In vitro assays showed substantial nitrate removal, with a net depletion of 455 mg L −1 NO 3 −1 . The nitrate removed from the medium was not recovered predominantly as extracellular or intracellular-associated NH 4 + . Although its specific fate cannot be determined from the present measurements, we hypothesize that it could have been assimilated with the bacteria in the form of amino acids. Soil column leaching experiments under contrasting soil types showed that coating or mixed fertilizers (granular NP, granular N, UAN) with CECT30572 significantly reduced cumulative NO 3 − leaching, with reductions of a magnitude similar to those observed with DMPP under several experimental conditions. Discussion Overall, these results indicate that the nitrate removal observed for B. subtilis CECT30572 under in vitro conditions may contribute to the reduction in nitrate losses observed in soil columns, supporting its potential as a bio-based strategy for reducing nitrate leaching.

### Short-term direct and indirect effects of herbicide application on an agricultural soil microbiome

Chemical herbicides, including glyphosate, atrazine, and many others, are commonly used to control unwanted vegetation in agroecosystems and protect crop yield potential. While the effects of herbicides on aboveground weedy vegetation are well documented, by comparison, the influence of herbicides on belowground processes is poorly understood. Given the importance of the soil microbiome for providing ecosystem services, understanding microbial responses to herbicide application is of pressing interest. In this study, the short-term non-target effects of herbicide application were examined by comparing three commonly used chemical herbicides, glyphosate, dicamba, and a tank mixture of atrazine + mesotrione, to handweeded and non-treated controls. Changes in soil microbial function, nutrient pools, and microbiome composition in Zea mays were assayed over 20 days post-treatment. Direct effects of the examined herbicides on soil edaphic properties, microbial alpha-diversity, and enzyme activities were small and transient over this 20-day, short-term experiment. Changes in enzymatic activities, nutrient concentrations, and fungal community structure were predicted primarily by weedy vegetation cover, suggesting that the observed responses are indirectly mediated by herbicide application. Conversely, weed removal treatment was a significant predictor of bacterial community composition, and chemical herbicide application was associated with homogenization of the bacterial community over the short sampling period, a trend not observed in the non-treated and handweeded controls. Together, these results indicate that in heavily managed agroecosystems, the short-term direct effects of chemical herbicide application on microbial diversity and enzymatic function are modest, whereas the effects on bacterial community assembly, composition, and heterogeneity are more readily detectable. The observed trend of biotic homogenization following pesticide application, a pattern not observed in the handweeded and non-treated controls, warrants further examination over longer time scales and repeated herbicide applications.

### Decoding microbial diversity in roots of rice plants under flooded conditions: influence of the host genotype, root compartment, and mycorrhizal association

ABSTRACT Rice is a vital staple for more than half of the world’s population, uniquely adapted to grow under flooded conditions. Our current understanding of root-associated microbiomes in rice plants under flooded conditions (anaerobic rice) is, however, limited. While rice plants are capable of establishing symbiotic associations with arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, also under flooded conditions, little is known about the impact that mycorrhizal associations might have on the root microbiome. Here, we used metagenomic sequencing to characterize the root-associated microbiomes of cultivated rice grown under flooded conditions. The root endosphere and rhizosphere compartments were found to harbor distinct microbial communities, with bacterial taxa comprising approximately 95% of the total microbial community. At the phylum level, the root bacteriome was primarily composed of Pseudomonadota (Alphaproteobacteria, Betaproteobacteria, and Gammaproteobacteria), followed by Actinomycetota. The fungal microbiome was dominated by Ascomycota (Sordariomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, and Dothideomycetes) and Basidiomycota. Both root compartment and host genotype significantly influenced microbiome composition, with most genotype-specific recruitment occurring at the species level. These associations were further reshaped in plants pre-inoculated with the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis . Co-occurrence networks revealed a genotype-dependent effect in the complexity of microbial taxa in mycorrhizal rice plants. Key microbial species primarily involved in methane production and nutrient cycling (e.g., phosphate-solubilizing bacteria and nitrogen-cycling bacteria) residing in roots of flooded rice were identified. Functional analysis of gene abundance related to those microbial communities revealed compartmentalization, with nutrient-cycling bacteria being more prevalent at the endosphere. Overall, this study demonstrated that the rice genotype, root compartment, and mycorrhizal condition shape the root microbiome in rice plants, ultimately influencing holobiont dynamics in flooded rice fields. IMPORTANCE Plant roots harbor a large diversity of microorganisms that strongly influence plant health and improve stress tolerance. Among them, root colonization by arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi enhances mineral nutrition and mitigates environmental stress. Although most rice cultivated worldwide is grown under anaerobic conditions (flooded fields), investigation of microbial communities inhabiting root-associated compartments of flooded rice, including AM fungi, has received only little attention. Here, we demonstrated that the rice genotype, root compartment, and mycorrhizal condition markedly influence the microbiome in roots of rice plants in flooded conditions, thus illustrating the potential of the rice plant to shape its root-associated microbiome. Differential root-associated microbiomes of commercial rice varieties include microbes primarily associated with methane production and nutrient cycling. Elucidation of factors influencing root-associated microbiomes in flooded rice systems, including their association with AM fungi, offers practical guidance for exploiting root-associated microbial communities for sustainable rice cultivation while reducing reliance on agrochemicals.

### Managing microbiomes in agroecosystems of the future

Optimal management of the plant microbiome would maximize the benefits of mutualistic symbionts and rhizosphere microorganisms, while minimizing damage from pathogens. Unfortunately, conventional agriculture degrades the plant microbiome, producing symbiont communities that are less beneficial to plant production. This chapter uses the context dependence of symbiont benefits and long-term dynamics of symbionts as a lens to understand the limited role of symbionts in modern conventional agriculture and to highlight the potential role of symbionts in a future agriculture. The authors consider not only the value of microbial inoculants, but also of other agroecology practices, such as polyculture, that can enhance the benefits of symbionts and rhizosphere microorganisms, while minimizing the costs of pathogens. Breeding crops to optimize value of improved microbiome is necessary to realize the best management. With better microbiome management, agroecosystems can be constructed to maintain productivity while reducing harm or even improving the environment.

### Study of Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi Population and Diversity in Robusta Coffee (Coffea canephora Pierre) Plantation in the District of West Lampung, Indonesia

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are beneficial fungi that occur naturally in the soil. However, their population and diversity may be affected by both biotic and abiotic factors. This study aimed to assess the population and diversity of AMF in the rhizosphere of Robusta coffee cultivated in the West Lampung District, Indonesia. Soil samples were taken from two coffee cropping systems: monocropping and mixed cropping. Samples were taken from seven points, each consisting of three coffee plants. For each plant, soil samples were collected from a depth of 20 cm at two points, each located 20 cm from the base of the coffee stem. The samples from the three coffee plants at each point were then composited to represent a single sample point. The wet sieving method was used to isolate AMF spores from the soil samples. AMF propagules in the soil samples were propagated using the trapping culture method with maize and sorghum as host plants. The results indicated that AMF spore abundance in mixed cropping (107.1 spores/50 g soil) was higher than in monocropped coffee (61.3 spores/50 g soil). From the trapping culture, five species of AMF were found in the mixed coffee cropping system (S1, S3, S4, S5, and S7), while six species (S1, S2, S3, S4, S6, and S7) were identified in the coffee monocropping system. AMF species S5 were present only in the mixed coffee cropping system, whereas species S2 and S6 were exclusive to the monocropping system.

### Multi-omics reveal dual phosphate-solubilizing mechanisms in sugarcane: bacterial gluconic acid secretion and host flavonoid modulation

While conventional phosphorus fertilizer application temporarily meets plant phosphorus demand, its long-term overuse has caused widespread and potentially irreversible ecological damage. Phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms (PSMs) are widely recognized as a sustainable alternative to enhance plant phosphorus acquisition; however, most studies to date have focused on screening PSMs for P-solubilizing traits, while a systematic characterization of the transcriptional and metabolic dynamics in sugarcane following PSM inoculation remains a critical and largely unaddressed knowledge gap. To address this gap, we integrated 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, plant growth-promoting trait assays, whole-genome analysis of bacterial isolates, host transcriptomics, untargeted metabolomics, rhizosphere microbiome profiling, and soil physicochemical and plant physiological analyses to comprehensively elucidate the mechanisms underlying PSM-mediated phosphorus uptake in sugarcane under varying phosphorus availability. First, through amplicon sequencing of sugarcane rhizosphere samples under high- and low-phosphorus conditions, we identified Gammaproteobacteria and Burkholderiales as core microbial taxa responsive to soil phosphorus levels. We subsequently isolated 21 culturable PSMs from the sugarcane rhizosphere and, after systematic validation of their P-solubilizing and plant growth-promoting traits, narrowed the selection to two elite strains that significantly enhanced sugarcane phosphorus uptake: Burkholderia seminalis Y2 and Pantoea sp. Y20. Functional and genomic analyses revealed that Y20 strain primarily solubilize inorganic phosphorus via gluconic acid secretion. Focusing on the superior strain Y20, we performed integrated multi-omics analyses to elucidate its multifaceted mode of action. Rhizosphere microbiome profiling showed that Y20 inoculation markedly reshaped the sugarcane rhizosphere microbial community, with a significantly increased relative abundance of beneficial genera, including Streptomyces , Lechevalieria , and Mycobacterium . Concurrently, Y20 inoculation substantially improved the rhizosphere phosphorus acquisition microenvironment by increasing soil organic matter and available phosphorus content, enhancing rhizosphere acid phosphatase activity, and ultimately improving key agronomic traits of sugarcane. Crucially, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses directly addressed the central knowledge gap by demonstrating that Y20 enabled sugarcane to cope with low- phosphorus stress through regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis, circadian rhythm, and genes involved in tropane and piperidine alkaloid biosynthetic pathways. Key flavonoid biosynthesis genes ( CHI , CHS , CYP75B1 , etc.) were significantly upregulated, accompanied by marked increases in phloretin, fustin, luteolin, and other flavonoid metabolites, thereby identifying flavonoid biosynthesis as a central component of the phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB)-mediated phosphorus uptake promotion mechanism. Collectively, this study provides the first systematic characterization of transcriptional and metabolic dynamics in sugarcane following PSB inoculation and, through integrated multi-omics approaches, comprehensively reveals the multidimensional mechanisms by which PSBs facilitate plant phosphorus acquisition. Our findings lay a solid theoretical foundation for the development of sustainable phosphorus management strategies and high-efficiency PSB-based microbial fertilizers for sugarcane production.
