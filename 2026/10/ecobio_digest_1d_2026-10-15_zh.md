### Silicate fertilizer mitigates methane emissions from rice paddies by enriching Burkholderia to modulate microbial interactions

Abstract Methane emissions from rice paddies are a major source of greenhouse gases, and silicate fertilizer (SF) derived from blast furnace slag has emerged as an environmentally friendly amendment that reduces methane release. SF modifies the soil redox environment by supplying electron acceptors such as iron, but the microbial mechanisms underlying methane mitigation remain unclear. Here, we combined metagenomic profiling, microbial isolation, and greenhouse experiments to show that SF consistently enriched Burkholderia vietnamiensis together with the methanotrophs in the rice rhizosphere. Inoculation of B. vietnamiensis strain HU01 alone reproduced the methane-suppressing effect of SF and increased methanotroph abundance. Cell-free culture supernatants of HU01 stimulated the growth of Methylococcus geothermalis and Methylocystis parvus through secreted, low-molecular-weight, heat-stable metabolites. Although metabolic modeling predicted primary metabolites such as L-glutamate and cobalamin as candidate cross-feeding factors, LC–MS/MS analysis of the highly active fractions indicated that the robust bioactivity was primarily driven by a chemically complex metabolite mixture rather than these ubiquitous single compounds. These results provide direct evidence that enrichment of a single heterotrophic species can enhance methane oxidation by stimulating methanotroph activity through metabolite exchange. B. vietnamiensis HU01 represents a promising microbial resource for methane mitigation and illustrates how targeted heterotroph–methanotroph interactions can be leveraged for climate-smart rice cultivation.

### Decomposition trajectories in mixed-species leaf litter in cool-temperate deciduous forest with understory dwarf bamboo

Litter decomposition plays a central role in nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems, but the effects of species mixing on decomposition of individual litter species remain poorly understood. We conducted a two-year litterbag experiment in a cool-temperate deciduous forest in northern Japan using litter from two dominant canopy tree species (oak and birch) and the dominant understory bamboo (Sasa). Single-species and mixed-species litterbags were used to examine litter mass loss, litter chemistry (carbon, nitrogen, and lignin), and microbial communities assessed by phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis. Oak and birch litter decomposed significantly faster than Sasa litter, whereas decomposition rate constants did not differ between the two deciduous tree species. Changes in litter chemistry relative to mass loss were similar between oak and birch but differed markedly in Sasa, indicating a distinct decomposition trajectory of Sasa. Total microbial biomass increased rapidly during the early stage of decomposition in all litter types owing to fungal colonization, whereas the fungal-to-bacterial PLFA ratio was substantially lower in Sasa litter than in deciduous tree litter. Species mixing had no significant overall non-additive effect on decomposition, although mixtures of oak and birch tended to accelerate decomposition, whereas mixtures including Sasa tended to suppress it. These changes in decomposition rate were accompanied by corresponding changes in microbial biomass, while decomposition trajectories remained largely unchanged within each litter species. Our results suggest that Sasa contributes a distinct decomposition process in cool-temperate forests and that species mixing primarily modifies decomposition rates rather than the sequence of chemical transformations during decomposition.

### Long-Established Monospecific Tree Stands are Associated with Distinct Soil Microbial Community Structure and Nutrient-Cycling Enzyme Activities in a Semi-Arid Gypsiferous Mediterranean Soil

Abstract This study assessed whether long-established monospecific stands of Eucalyptus camaldulensis (EC), Eucalyptus occidentalis (EO), Pinus halepensis (PH), and Cupressus sempervirens (CS), established approximately 70 years ago, were associated with distinct patterns of soil microbial community structure and biochemical functioningin a semi-arid Mediterranean gypsiferous soil. Specifically, we examined differences in soil organic matter pools, microbial community structure, and potential enzyme activities related to C, N, and P cycling, and explored their associations with soil resource status and leaf/needle chemical traits. Mineral topsoil, leaves or needles, and litter were sampled from monospecific stands established on the same Gypsic Haploxerept near San Cataldo, Sicily. Soil chemical and biochemical properties were assessed, including microbial biomass C and N, respiration, microbial community structure, and potential enzyme activities. In addition, the elemental composition and fiber fractions of leaves and litter were analyzed. Differences among stands were assessed by one-way ANOVA, while exploratory stepwise discriminant function analyses were used to integrate soil, microbial, enzymatic, and plant-trait variables. The four stands differed markedly in soil organic matter pools, microbial community structure, and biochemical functioning. EC was associated with greater C and N availability, highest microbial biomass, respiration, and β-glucosidase activity, indicating greater potential for microbial C acquisition. PH and CS showed highest phosphatase activity consistent with greater potential for P acquisition, while their microbial profiles showed relatively greater contributions of fungal and/or actinomycete phospholipid fatty acid markers. EO showed a distinct profile, combining relatively high organic C availability with comparatively low urease and phosphatase activities. Exploratory discriminant analyses indicated that both soil resource status and residue structural traits contributed to stand separation. Long-established monospecific stands were associated with distinct soil microbial community structure and biochemical profile in this gypsiferous Mediterranean soil. Integrating enzyme activities, phospholipid fatty acid profiles, and plant-residue chemistry helped characterize these differences, which should be interpreted as associations rather than replicated causal species effects.

### Soil nitrogen dynamics in riparian zones of two Arctic rivers

Riparian zones act as protective buffers, filtering land-to-river nutrient inputs through retention and nitrogen (N) gas losses. While rapid Arctic warming and permafrost thaw may enhance terrestrial nutrient inputs to aquatic systems, comprehensive assessments of N cycling in Arctic riparian soils are lacking. Conventionally, these zones are considered inactive due to N limitation of Arctic soils and low temperatures, leaving their N cycling unexplored. To address this, we investigated N pools and turnover processes in riparian zones of two Arctic rivers in Finland and Greenland. We combined measurements of the extractable N pool and in-situ nitrous oxide (N2O) flux with 15N-labeling techniques to quantify gross mineralization, nitrification, potential denitrification, and potential dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA). Our findings reveal that mineral N cycling in high-latitude riparian zones is more active than previously assumed. Despite low temperatures and low to intermediate soil organic carbon content, we found high gross N mineralization rates, alongside detectable rates of gross nitrification and potential DNRA. We attribute the unexpectedly high N activity in C-poor soils to low C/N ratios, which create a stoichiometric imbalance, thereby enhancing N mineralization. The presence or absence of vegetation largely determined the dominance and magnitude of these pathways. While dense vascular plant cover was associated with higher extractable N pools and gross mineralization rate, low nitrification rates and elevated DNRA potential indicated a tight N cycle. Conversely, sparsely vegetated patches with cryptogamic biocrusts acted as N2O hotspots. In bare sandy soils adjacent to the river, we found high gross nitrification rate and detectable N2O losses, possibly driven by riverine organic N inputs. Taken together, this research shows that Arctic riparian zones may play an important role in reducing terrestrial N inputs to the rivers, which should be considered when studying the fate of N in the rapidly changing Arctic.

### Transcriptomic Response of Medicago lupulina MlS-1 Roots to Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi with Contrasting Symbiotic Efficiency

Arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM) plays a crucial role in agroecosystems. The molecular mechanisms by which host plants regulate symbiosis efficiency remain poorly understood. This study compared root transcriptomic responses of mycotrophic Medicago lupulina MlS-1 to effective Rhizophagus irregularis RCAM00320 versus ineffective Glomus sp. 129.1Te under phosphorus deficiency. RNA-Seq analysis revealed that the effective symbiont induced a large-scale, coordinated transcriptional reprogramming, characterized by targeted upregulation of symbiotic phosphate and ammonium transporters, strong induction of genes for lipid mobilization (the triacylglycerol lipase gene LIP) and the structural protein gene (Oleosin) for lipid droplet stabilization, to support fungal nutrition, and a strategy of controlled cell wall remodeling that minimized the release of immunogenic damage-associated molecular patterns. In contrast, the ineffective strain triggered only a fragmented, low-amplitude stress response, accompanied by hormonal deregulation and activation of defense-related genes, suggesting plant mechanisms to restrict resources to non-functional symbionts. The identified gene set (potential AM efficiency markers) has candidate markers with potential diagnostic value for screening AM inoculants and breeding mycotrophic legumes.

### Research Progress on the Regulation of Plant Rhizosphere Environment Using Nanomaterials

With unique physicochemical properties, nanomaterials offer new pathways for precisely regulating the plant rhizosphere environment and advancing sustainable agriculture. This review synthesizes 115 studies published between 2016 and 2026, focusing on how nanomaterials reshape the “microbe–soil–plant” ternary interaction network through multiple mechanisms, including the modulation of rhizosphere microbial functions, the driving of soil elemental cycles, and the regulation of root physiological and molecular responses. We also highlight the pronounced disconnect between experimental doses and environmentally relevant concentrations, and emphasize the need for field-scale validation. Further in-depth research is required to elucidate the interaction mechanisms and environmental behaviors at multiple rhizosphere interfaces, advance the green application of nanomaterials, and ultimately facilitate the construction of a precise, safe, and sustainable nano-agricultural technology system.

### Editorial: Microbiome-driven strategies for alleviation of biotic and abiotic stresses in agriculture

Over recent decades, advances in plant microbiome research have transformed our understanding of plants as complex biological systems integrated with diverse microbial communities, whose interactions shape the functioning and fitness of the plant holobiont (Berg et al., 2016). This conceptual shift is reinforced by evidence that plant-associated microbiomes are shaped by host, environmental, and management factors, including biotic and abiotic stresses, and, in turn, influence plant performance through nutrient acquisition, phytohormone regulation, pathogen suppression, stress signaling, and host physiological responses (Trivedi et al., 2020). Building on these advances, plant-microbe research is moving beyond taxonomic profiling toward understanding microbiome assembly and function, while exploring synthetic communities and targeted microbiome manipulation for predictable, persistent field benefits. In this context, this Research Topic brings together 11 contributions that provide complementary perspectives on how plant-associated microbial communities respond to biotic and abiotic stresses, how their assembly and functions are shaped by the host and its environment, and how a deeper understanding of these interactions may help harness beneficial microbiome functions for crop resilience.Among the themes explored in this Research Topic, the relationship between resident microbial communities and plant disease offers compelling evidence of the plant microbiome's functional importance. The capacity of indigenous microbiota to suppress pathogen establishment and disease development was recognized through the phenomenon of disease-suppressive soils (Cook & Rovira, 1976), with subsequent community-level analyses identifying microbial taxa and interactions associated with such suppression (Mendes et al., 2011). Amplicon-based profiling still predominates, underscoring the need for greater integration of shotgun metagenomics and functional multi-omics to resolve underlying mechanisms. Hibatullah et al. (2026) similarly emphasized integrating multi-omics-guided rhizobiome engineering with regenerative soil management, while highlighting the need for long-term field validation. Crucially, associations between microbial taxa and biotic or abiotic stress resistance do not establish causality, nor do co-occurrence networks confirm direct microbial interactions. Zhao et al. Longitudinal studies across host genotypes, cropping cycles, environments, and management regimes are needed to establish the stability and context dependence of microbiome functions. Cross-kingdom interactions among bacteria, fungi, archaea, protists, and viruses remain underexplored, particularly their collective roles in pathogen suppression, nutrient cycling, and functional resilience. Bridging these gaps requires integrating community profiling with microbial isolation, re-inoculation, gnotobiotic models, synthetic communities, and functional assays to validate microbial interactions and develop predictable, field-reproducible strategies for crop resilience.
