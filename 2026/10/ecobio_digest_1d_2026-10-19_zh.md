### Warming-Driven Mercury Evasion from Northern Permafrost Peatlands (Hg distribution)

Boreal permafrost peatlands harbour vast mercury (Hg) pools accumulated over millennia. However, the vulnerability of this legacy Hg to ongoing climate warming remains poorly understood. Here, we compiled a comprehensive soil Hg dataset from 923 locations within the permafrost zone and established a 13-year field warming experiment in a permafrost peatland. Additionally, we conducted controlled laboratory incubations to investigate the microbial mechanisms underlying Hg dynamics in response to warming. The results showed that soil Hgconcentrations in the southern permafrost regions of Eurasia were significantly higher than those in the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and other boreal permafrost regions, thereby increasing the environmental risk of Hg migration. Crucially, concordant evidence from field warming experiments and laboratory incubations shows that while warming does not stimulate Hg methylation potential, it substantially facilitates demethylation, driving enhanced volatilisation of gaseous Hg (Hg0) into the atmosphere. Rising temperatures reduced bacterial and fungal diversity, accelerated soil organic matter decomposition, and simultaneously promoted the diversity and network connectivity of demethylating microorganisms. Microbial communities harbouring the demethylation-related merAgene were predominantly identified as methanogens (Methanomassiliicoccales) and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (Thioalkalivibrio). Our findings highlight that microbially mediated decomposition may mobilize Hgand greenhouse gases to a greater extent than previously estimated. This underscores the urgency of incorporating permafrost thaw dynamics into pan-Arctic mercury budget assessments to capture the response of the Hg cycle to accelerating climate change.

### Simulating N2O emissions following crop residue return while preserving consistency at the crop succession scale: A multi-year field assessment of the STICS model

Effective mitigation of agricultural greenhouse gas emissions requires improved prediction of how management practices affect soil nitrous oxide (N 2 O) emissions. This study aimed to evaluate the ability of the STICS model to simulate the response of the N2O emissions to crop-residue return events, while preserving the model performance at the crop-succession scale. Data from eight arable systems in a long-term experiment, which generated variations in the amount and composition of residues returned to soil, were used to calibrate the denitrification module of STICS and to evaluate performance on an independent dataset. Two model versions were compared: the standard one and a modified version accounting for the effect of soluble compounds released during residue decomposition on denitrification potential. Model performance was assessed both at the scale of crop successions and over 60-day periods following residue return. STICS simulated 10-year cumulative N 2 O emissions accurately for most systems. However, simulation of emissions linked to residue management remained inconstant, even after calibration focused on post-return periods. The modified version of the STICS model yet produced responses to residue incorporation consistent with those reported in the literature and improved simulation of certain residue return events. Its limited influence on improving performance at the crop succession scale or across all return events confirms the observed rather low sensitivity of N 2 O emissions to residue management at this site, which is likely to be due to inherent soil or residue properties.

### Root exudates-microbe interactions promote nodulation in legume-based intercropping

The classical nitrogen competition hypothesis posits that intercropping enhances legume nodulation as a passive response to reduced soil N availability. We propose a complementary mechanism whereby nitrogen competition serves as an ecological trigger, while root exudate-mediated microbial restructuring may amplify symbiotic interactions. In a meta-analysis of 157 studies, we found that intercropping selectively altered flavonoid and organic acid exudation in a crop-combination-dependent manner, with baseline exudation levels of monocultured legumes emerging as an important negative moderator. Intercropping consistently promoted arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal (AMF) colonization and enriched key plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) taxa, particularly Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas, with stronger microbial responses under low soil nitrogen. Co-inoculation with AMF and rhizobia or multiple PGPR strains generally produced greater nodulation benefits than single-strain inoculation, although these benefits declined with increasing intrinsic nodulation potential, indicating host-imposed constraints on symbiotic enhancement. Integrating these patterns with mechanistic insights, our synthesis indicates that reciprocal interactions between root exudates and rhizosphere microbes may reinforce rhizobial infection, phytohormone signalling, and nutrient mobilization, thereby sustaining and amplifying nodulation. This framework extends the resource-competition paradigm and provides a basis for developing crop combinations and microbial management strategies to enhance symbiotic N inputs in sustainable intercropping systems.

### How agricultural legacy shapes the drought response of the wheat rhizobiome

Abstract Background Drought is an increasing threat to soil microbiomes, which underpin critical soil functions and crop production. While drought-induced shifts in soil microbial diversity are well documented, the impact on the functional potential of microbiomes remains poorly understood. It is particularly unclear whether alternative cropping systems, such as organic farming, enhance the ability of microbes to maintain their functionality under drought compared to conventional systems. To fill this gap, we imposed a field-scale drought in a 44-year long-term trial comparing organic and conventional systems, and assessed the functional potential of the wheat rhizosphere microbiome using shotgun metagenomics. Results Agricultural management legacy was the primary driver of the rhizosphere functional gene composition, accounting for 24–32% of total variation across ontologies and persisting under drought (which explained 3–6% of variation). While the magnitude of the drought-induced shift in the functional potential was similar across all systems, drought-stressed communities remained distinct. All systems enriched metagenomic toolkits for core drought endurance, including cell membrane modification, endospore formation, osmolyte accumulation, antioxidant production, and DNA repair. Conversely, we observed a relative depletion of genes associated with ribosomal hibernation (dormancy) and exopolysaccharide synthesis under drought, suggesting a reliance on plant root exudates over endogenous genomic investment. In terms of the metabolic potential, communities exhibited an enriched capacity for inorganic phosphorus and iron scavenging alongside a relative depletion of genes involved in complex organic matter degradation. Although communities across all systems shared a common set of functional genes associated with stress survival and metabolic adaptation, they exhibited nuanced system-specific metagenomic strategies, marked by an enrichment of sporulation-related genes in conventional systems receiving synthetic inputs and programmed-cell-death pathways in manure-fertilized systems. Conclusion Our findings reveal that agricultural management plays a dual role: profoundly shaping the functional potential of the soil microbiome while exerting limited influence on the core metagenomic toolkit for drought adaptation, with only subtle nuances between systems. Rather than driving broad system-specific shifts, drought stress consistently enriched metagenomic toolkits associated with osmotic stress tolerance, cellular maintenance, and nutrient scavenging across all management regimes. Ultimately, no cropping system conferred superior metagenomic resistance to drought, yet each system preserved its distinct metagenomic identity under severe water deficit, highlighting the enduring legacy of agricultural management.

### Evaluation of Salt-Tolerant Rhizobacteria for Sustainable Plant Growth and Soil Health Improvement

Soil saline is one of the major abiotic stresses that severely reduced agricultural productivity as it imposes growth, nutrient and soil fertility constraints. This also reduces microbial diversity and, therefore, the nutrient cycle that is required for maintaining soil health. Recently, eco-friendly and sustainable salt-tolerant rhizobacteria have been developed to treat salinity stress. They promote plant growth by nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, production of phytohormones, activity of ACC deaminase, exopoly saccharides synthesis and enhanced antioxidant defense. In addition to crop growth, soil conditioners promote the availability of nutrients through increased solubility and improved activity of soil micro-biota, all contributing to a more effective increase in soil quality. Plant growth promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) can mitigate or ameliorate salt-stress and give an eco-friendly sustainable approach against the use of synthetic fertilizers. We further summarize recent research efforts, possible applications so far, challenges and future prospects for utilization of these beneficial microorganisms in sustainable agriculture.
