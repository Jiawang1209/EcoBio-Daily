### One-step microbial conversion of waste molasses into bioactive fulvic-like acid fertilizer for sustainable soil quality improvement and agricultural waste valorization

Waste molasses (WM), a carbon rich by-product of sugar production, is frequently underutilized, resulting in environmental pollution and resource loss. In this study, a one-step microbial bioconversion process using Bacillus subtilis was developed to transform WM into molasses derived fulvic acid (MFA). The highest MFA yield (33.54%) was achieved at 35 °C, 48 h, and a 5% inoculation rate. Integrated GC-MS and LC-MS analyses revealed dynamic substrate transformation during fermentation and suggested that 4-hydroxy-2-methylacetophenone, 4-oxododecanedioic acid, and 3-hydroxy-2-octylpentanedioic acid were putative intermediates involved in MFA formation. Structural characterization showed that the resulting MFA possessed a loose, porous morphology and abundant hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl groups. In a single 150 d field trial, MFA application was associated with higher soil organic carbon and humus contents than the unfertilized control and promoted maize vegetative growth. MFA also enriched the microbial communities including Pseudomonas , Acidobacterium , Actinobacteria , and Bacteroidetes . The abundance of several genes related to carbon assimilation and carboxylation, including rbcL , rbcS , accB , accC , and pccA , has also increased. Metabolomic analysis further revealed shifts in metabolites associated with central carbon metabolic pathways, including glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway MFA application also markedly promoted maize growth without chemical fertilizer input, demonstrating its potential as an organic soil amendment and partial fertilizer substitute. Collectively, these findings suggest that a one-step microbial transformation can convert WM into functional fulvic-like acid fertilizer. This method provides a potential route for mineral derived FA production. Simultaneously combining the reuse of agricultural waste, improvement of soil quality, reduction of partial fertilizer dependence, and development of circular agriculture.

### Azospirillum brasilense and cytidine as combination treatment to enhance drought resilience of peas

Aims: This study tested the efficacy of two combination treatments on drought resistance of peas; Azospirillum brasilense Sp7 together with either the chemoattractant seedling exudate compound cytidine or total pea seedling exudate. Methods and Results: Phenotypic characterization revealed that the treatment with cytidine (ACyt) was more efficient than that with seedling exudate (AEx). Drought tolerance of peas treated with ACyt was 68.9% higher than that of peas treated with the inoculant alone (A). Dry shoot weight of ACyt Drought plants was 30.7% higher than that of plants from the untreated control group NB Drought, drought recovery of ACyt plants was 21.6% better. 16S rDNA analysis revealed that increase in drought tolerance/recovery was accompanied by modulation of the rhizosphere. The abundance of the inoculant was 163% higher in ACyt plants when comparing drought treated to watered groups. Abundance of Azospirillum sp. was 954% higher in plants from the ACyt Drought group than those from NB Drought. The possibility arises that the ACyt treatment may contribute to protecting pea plants from drought by increasing the abundance of Azospirillum. Within the complete analysis of the 16S rDNA data set, both ACyt Drought and AEx Drought groups had similar increases in Shannon and Pielou alpha-diversity indices, relative to the NB Drought group. These two test groups also exhibited high abundances of Rhizobium and lower abundance of Massilia. Conclusion: The ACyt treatment enhanced drought tolerance and recovery. Both treatments enhanced alpha-diversity and modulated the rhizosphere.

### Harnessing Winery Sludge Compost for Sustainable Viticulture: Effects on Soil Fertility and Microbial Activity in Pot and Field Trials

The economic significance of the viticulture and winemaking sectors has led to the intensification and specialization of grape production systems. However, the extensive use of chemical fertilizers has contributed to declining soil fertility, a problem further exacerbated by climate change. In this context, organic amendments derived from waste materials offer a sustainable strategy for improving soil quality and fertility features, in line with circular economy principles. This study evaluated the effects of compost derived from winery sludges (WSC) on soil fertility and rhizosphere microbial activity in vineyards, under both semi-controlled (pot experiment) and field conditions. Soil enzyme activities, community-level physiological profiling (CLPP) and microbial community composition were assessed to characterize microbial functional responses. The pot trial was conducted on cv. Cabernet Sauvignon (Vitis vinifera L.) grafted on Kober 5BB (V. berlandieri x V. riparia) grown in 80 litres pots. The field trial was carried out using cv. Sangiovese (V. vinifera L.) vines grafted on 110 Richter (V. berlandieri x V. rupestris). Plants were organized in a randomized block design and treated with mineral fertilization, WSC and unfertilised control. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected at flowering, veraison and harvest. In the pot trial, alkaline phosphatase activity was generally higher than acid phosphatase activity. This enzyme showed significantly greater activity in mineral-treated samples at flowering, while compost-treated samples exhibited higher activity at harvest. Urease activity followed a similar pattern. In contrast, β-glucosidase activity in WSC-treated samples declined over the season. In the field trial, WSC application significantly increased the activities of phosphatases, β-glucosidase and arylsulfatase from flowering to harvest. CLPP analysis in the pot experiment showed that mineral fertilizer had a greater effect on microbial activity at flowering. However, at harvest, the trend reversed, with WSC exerting a stronger influence on microbial functional diversity. A similar trend was observed in the field trial, where WSC promoted broader microbial functionality at harvest. The study of the microbial community composition is currently ongoing and will be performed through metabarcoding sequencing of 16S and ITS. This will enable to have a deeper insight on the impact of WSC. Overall, these preliminary findings suggest that, regardless of the cultivation method or grape cultivar, the application of WSC can enhance soil fertility and microbial activity, exerting a sustained and progressively increasing effect throughout the growing season.

### Succinate treatment reduces bacterial wilt disease and modulates the viable soil microbiota in tomato

Abstract Succinate is an intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and is well known for its role in energy metabolism. However, succinate is also involved in diverse cellular signaling processes in various organisms and may also play signaling roles in the plant microbiome. Previously, we showed that succinate acts as a public good in microbial syntrophy between an unculturable beneficiary and a helper bacterium, supporting the viability of energy-auxotrophic bacteria and their wide distribution in soil. In this study, we examined whether succinate treatment manages tomato bacterial wilt and modulates the viable soil microbiota. When tomato plants were treated with succinate together with an upland soil microbial fraction, bacterial wilt disease was significantly reduced. To assess the impact of exogenous succinate on bulk and rhizosphere soil microbiota, we performed microbiome analyses using propidium monoazide to focus on viable bacterial communities. Succinate treatment significantly increased microbial diversity in both bulk and rhizosphere soil microbiota, enriching Nocardioidaceae, Sphingomonadaceae, and Paenibacillaceae in bulk soil and Sphingomonadaceae and Erythrobacteraceae in the tomato rhizosphere. Based on microbiome data, we constructed a succinate-responsive synthetic community (SynCom) comprising six bacterial strains isolated from upland soil. Interestingly, the SynCom suppressed bacterial wilt only when co-applied with succinate in tomato plants. Our findings indicate that exogenous succinate can be used as a novel bioprotectant to manage bacterial wilt by enriching viable beneficial soil microbiota, offering a promising strategy for sustainable agriculture.

### Potential application of rhizobacteria as bio-inoculants for enhancing growth of edamame [Glycine max (L.) Merr.]

Edamame [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a crop of significant economic and nutritional value, offering notable health benefits. This study aimed to evaluate the plant growth-promoting traits of rhizobacteria under in vitro conditions and to evaluate the effects of a potential strain on pod-related agronomic traits in edamame. All of the 13 tested isolates exhibited the ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen, solubilize inorganic phosphate, and produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). The application of Bacillus albus AG3.1 as a soil drench allowed for a 25%–50% reduction in nitrogen fertilizer input relative to the full-fertilizer control, while maintaining comparable plant growth performance. These findings highlight B. albus AG3.1 as a promising candidate for the development of microbial bioinoculants aimed at promoting environmentally friendly and fertilizer-efficient edamame cultivation.
