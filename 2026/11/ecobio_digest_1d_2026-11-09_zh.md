### Plant invasion alters rhizosphere microbial communities across temperate ecosystems

Invasive plants threaten ecosystems, and their management remains a challenge in ecological restoration. Control and replanting outcomes can vary considerably, with restoration sites often experiencing re-invasion, secondary invasion, or failed native plant establishment. While impacts are often focused above ground, plant invasion can also alter rhizosphere microbial communities in ways that may inhibit recovery. This study characterized bacterial and fungal communities of native and invasive shrub-layer plants across three British Columbia ecosystems (coastal forest, riparian forest, and coastal wetland) and tested whether invasion-associated taxonomic shifts corresponded with differences in predicted microbial functions and ecological guilds. Rhizosphere soils were sequenced for 16S rRNA and ITS2 markers and analyzed in QIIME2 using weighted UniFrac and PERMANOVA. Functional potential (PICRUSt2 MetaCyc pathways) and fungal guilds (FUNGuild) were compared using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities and PERMANOVA. Microbial communities differed significantly by plant species and invasion status in the riparian site, by plant species in the coastal forest site, and showed no significant differences with lower amplicon sequence variant (ASV) richness at the coastal wetland site. Predicted functional composition differed among plant species in coastal forest bacterial MetaCyc profiles, while fungal functional groups differed by both plant species and invasion status at the riparian site, where invasive Japanese knotweed showed a predicted shift from ectomycorrhizal toward saprotrophic fungi. Responses were strongly context-dependent across ecosystems. These findings highlight the importance of belowground microbial variation in invasion ecology and support further investigation on whether targeted soil interventions, such as inoculation or soil transfer, can improve restoration outcomes.

### Panax notoginseng residue fertilizer drives tobacco quality enhancement via rhizosphere multi-omics cascades

The potential of using traditional Chinese medicine residues as organic fertilizers to improve crop quality has attracted increasing attention, yet the underlying soil microecological mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of Panax notoginseng residue composite organic fertilizer on tobacco quality formation. A field experiment was conducted with three treatments: no organic fertilizer (CK), traditional farmyard manure (CL_1), and P. notoginseng residue fertilizer (CL_2). Agronomic traits, leaf chemical compositions, and sensory qualities were evaluated, integrated with soil multi-omics analysis to characterize the rhizospheric microbial community. The results demonstrated that P. notoginseng residue fertilizer significantly optimized the quality and output value structure of tobacco. Compared with the CK soil, P. notoginseng residue fertilizer increased the yield of premium-grade leaves by 38%. Chemically, P. notoginseng residue fertilizer modulated the foliar carbon-nitrogen balance, elevating total sugars and free amino acids, while accelerating the enrichment of key secondary metabolites such as chlorogenic acid, rutin, and scopolin. These adjustments enhanced sensory scores by reducing irritation and masking off-flavors. Soil multi-omics revealed that P. notoginseng residue fertilizer elevated hydrolyzable nitrogen and available potassium, directionally enriching core functional bacteria such as Pseudoxanthomonas , Pseudomonas , and Lysobacter . In conclusion, the P. notoginseng residue fertilizer orchestrates tobacco quality enhancement by improving soil nutrient availability and recruiting specialized microorganisms, which collectively drive foliar aroma precursor biosynthesis. These findings demonstrate that recycling P. notoginseng residue into composite fertilizer is a promising strategy for achieving high-quality tobacco production and sustainable management of agricultural waste.

Abstract. The oxygen isotopic composition of nitrate (NO₃⁻) produced by nitrification (δ¹⁸ONO3,nit) establishes the initial isotopic signature of NO₃⁻ in environmental systems and provides a baseline for tracing NO₃⁻ sources and subsequent transformations. However, how δ¹⁸ONO3,nit and its relationship with water δ¹⁸O (δ¹⁸OH2O) vary with temperature and substrate availability in agricultural soils remains poorly constrained. Moreover, soil evaporation can enrich δ18OH2O in surface soils where nitrification is most active, yet how this enrichment influences δ¹⁸ONO3,nit under contrasting hydrological conditions remains unexplored. To address these gaps, we integrated soil incubation experiments with a synthetic transport modeling framework based on StorAge Selection (SAS) functions to quantify how biogeochemical and hydrological factors regulate the variability and predictability of δ¹⁸ONO3,nit in an agricultural soil. The incubation experiments showed that, across wide ranges of temperature (7–35 °C) and ammonium (NH4+) availability (0–98 mg N kg⁻¹ soil), δ¹⁸ONO3,nit generally tracked soil δ¹⁸OH2O but varied by 3.4–5.3 ‰ at a given δ¹⁸OH2O. Temperature and NH4+ availability influenced δ¹⁸ONO3,nit primarily by regulating transient nitrite (NO2⁻) accumulation, which enhanced NO2⁻–water oxygen isotope exchange and thereby altered the expression of the associated equilibrium isotope effect. Synthetic transport modeling further revealed that hydrological processes can generate δ¹⁸ONO3,nit variability comparable in magnitude to that produced by biogeochemical controls. Because δ¹⁸ONO3,nit records the δ¹⁸O of water in nitrification zones at the time of NO₃⁻ production, whereas drainage δ18OH2O is additionally shaped by the mixing of water transported through heterogeneous flow paths, using drainage δ18OH2O to predict δ¹⁸ONO3,nit can produce systematic biases, particularly when seasonal nitrification dynamics interact with soil evaporation and water transport. Overall, our results show that δ¹⁸ONO3,nit in soil is jointly controlled by biogeochemical processes that regulate the expression of kinetic and equilibrium isotope effects during nitrification and by hydrological processes that determine the sources, ages, and flow paths of water supplying nitrification. These findings underscore the need to move beyond stream-centric approaches toward reactive transport frameworks that explicitly accounts for dynamic biogeochemical controls across diverse flow paths and water ages when applying NO₃⁻ isotopes to environmental systems.

### Effect of water management on nitrous oxide emission and heterotrophic soil respiration from drained grassland on peat soil

Abstract Raising groundwater levels in drained peatlands used for dairy farming aims to reduce peat decomposition, land subsidence and associated carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions. However, higher groundwater levels also affect soil biological nitrogen (N) transformations by decreasing N mineralization and influencing microbial processes such as nitrification and denitrification, which may affect nitrous oxide (N₂O) emissions. This creates a potential trade-off, where reduced peat mineralization and CO₂ release may coincide with higher N₂O emissions. We quantified the effects of ditch water level and water infiltration systems (WIS) on N₂O emissions from peat grassland in a five-year field experiment in the Netherlands. Four peat grassland fields differed in ditch water level (20 vs 55 cm below the soil surface) and were subdivided into control without infiltration, passive WIS (PWIS) and active WIS (AWIS) treatments. N₂O fluxes were measured weekly using closed chambers and gap-filled with a generalized additive model. Averaged over five years, deep ditch levels resulted in higher dry matter yield (10.3 vs 8.8 t DM ha ⁻1 yr ⁻1 ) and plant N uptake (282 vs 241 kg N ha ⁻1 yr ⁻1 ), indicating higher N turnover. Annual cumulative N₂O emissions were also higher under deep ditch conditions (3.6 vs 1.9 kg N₂O–N ha ⁻1 yr ⁻1 ), whereas WIS had no significant effect on N 2 O emissions. In contrast, heterotrophic soil respiration was reduced by 10% under PWIS and 20% under AWIS compared to the control. These findings show that raising ditch water levels can reduce N₂O emissions by up to 47%, offering a promising water management strategy to mitigate N₂O losses with only minor yield penalties.

### Evaluating the decomposition of agricultural legume species to estimate nutrient recycling in Mediterranean climates

Legumes enhance the sustainability of Mediterranean farming systems by increasing soil nutrient levels, improving soil water and carbon retention, and reducing reliance on synthetic fertilisers through biological nitrogen fixation. However, the environmental and biological complexity of residue decomposition necessitates a clear understanding of how different crop residues decompose in synchrony with the nutrient requirements of subsequent cereal or oilseed crops. To address this, litterbag experiments were conducted at two distinct agroecological zones within the Western Australian Wheatbelt (Kendenup: 730 mm rainfall, Brown Gravelly Chromosol; Konnongorring: 350 mm rainfall, Grey Chromosol sandy duplex). Residues from nine legume species (grain legumes: Lupinus angustifolius, Vicia faba ; traditional pastures: Trifolium subterraneum, Vicia sativa, V. villosa ; novel ley legumes: Ornithopus sativus, Trigonella balansae, Trifolium spumosum ) and wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) were monitored from summer to winter (60 weeks) to assess decomposition patterns and associated C and N dynamics. Decomposition rates were significantly higher at the high-rainfall Kendenup site, with all species showing 70–85% biomass loss by 60 weeks compared to 50–75% at Konnongorring, reflecting moisture as the primary driver of decomposition. Across both sites, T. aestivum exhibited the slowest decomposition and highest N immobilisation (final C:N ~ 35), followed by grain legumes L. angustifolius and V. faba (final C:N 30–40). Vetch species ( V. sativa, V. villosa ) and T. spumosum decomposed most rapidly, achieving final C:N ratios of 11–19. Novel ley legumes O. sativus and Trig. balansae displayed intermediate decomposition rates and achieved a C:N ratio of 25–35, potentially optimising N release synchrony with subsequent crop demand. These findings provide the first comparative assessment of second-generation ley legumes ( O. sativus , Trig. balansae ) alongside traditional pasture and grain legumes under field conditions in Mediterranean environments. Results demonstrate that novel pasture legumes provided sustained N release, potentially optimising nutrient synchrony with subsequent cereal crops better than either fast-decomposing traditional pastures or low N fixer grain legume residues. This species-specific decomposition information addresses a critical knowledge gap in managing N cycling in evolving Australian farming systems, where increases in N harvest index and changes to ley systems have altered N dynamics. This study demonstrates that, while edaphoclimatic conditions were strongly associated with residue decomposition in the Mediterranean environments of Western Australia, species selection substantially influenced the timing of N release. Novel ley legumes offer intermediate decomposition patterns that may better balance availability and consistency of N release compared to traditional legume options, with potential implications for managing nutrient synchrony, cereal biomass, and grain quality (protein and grain size) in evolving rotational systems.
