### Sampling time outweighs compost and vegetation cover effects on soil fertility and microbial communities in a Mediterranean olive orchard

Abstract
                  
                    Organic amendments and vegetation covers are increasingly promoted to improve soil fertility and biological functioning in Mediterranean perennial crops, yet their effects occur within highly dynamic soil environments. We evaluated the temporal and management-related variation of soil physicochemical properties, enzyme activities, and bacterial, fungal and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal (AMF) communities in an organically managed Mediterranean olive orchard. A factorial field experiment combined compost application with spontaneous or sown vegetation cover was assessed at two sampling times. Sampling time was the main source of variation in the soil physicochemical profile (R² = 0.299,
                    P
                     = 0.001), whereas compost exerted a significant effect only at the short-term sampling (R² = 0.191,
                    P
                     = 0.023). Enzyme activities were also strongly time-dependent, although compost increased dehydrogenase and urease activities. Microbial responses differed among groups: bacterial alpha-diversity showed no significant temporal changes, fungal richness increased, and AMF richness declined despite an increase in Hill-Shannon diversity. Temporal shifts in community composition were more pronounced for bacteria and fungi than for AMF. Compost influenced bacterial community composition, while its effects on AMF diversity depended on sampling time. Vegetation cover did not induce significant community-wide compositional shifts, although its effects on AMF diversity also varied with time. Medium-term treatment-associated indicator ASVs revealed treatment-specific associations, including contrasting AMF genera under different vegetation and compost treatments. Overall, temporal dynamics outweighed broad management effects, while compost and vegetation cover generated selective changes in microbial composition and biochemical functioning. These results highlight the value of integrating physicochemical, biochemical and microbial indicators across time to distinguish transient responses from persistent management effects in Mediterranean agroecosystems.

### Spatiotemporal Dynamics and Environmental Drivers of Maize Microbiome Structure across Diverse Agroecosystems in Georgia, USA

The understanding of how microbial communities differ across soils, environments, plant tissues, and developmental stages is key to the improvement of crop productivity and resilience. Here, we conducted a statewide survey of maize (Zea mays L.) microbiomes across 35 fields at 16 locations in Georgia, USA, sampling soil, rhizosphere, root interior, and stalk interior at two developmental stages (600 and 1400 growing degree days, GDD). More than 38000 ASVs were produced after amplicon sequencing of 16S rRNA genes. The phyla shown to be dominant are Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Planctomycetota, and Acidobacteriota. Based on alpha and beta diversity analyses, the below-ground compartments (soil and rhizosphere) revealed higher diversity and stronger spatial structuring when compared to the above-ground sample types, whereas root and stalk interior microbiomes revealed marked shifts across growth stages. Amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and energy-related pathways predictions (via PICRUSt2) show stage-dependent enrichment. Network analyses show persistent keystone taxa (Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota) mediating microbial stability, coupled with an increase in modularity and connectivity as we move from early to late growth stages. Some environmental variables, such as temperature, humidity, soil potassium, and soil sodium, showed significant correlation with microbial diversity indicative of a compartment-specific environmental sensitivity. Collectively, our results show that in maize, microbial community composition, interaction, and potential functionality are both impacted by developmental stages and environmental heterogeneity. This study provides a fundamental framework for understanding the microbiome dynamics in field-grown maize across various agroecological conditions. It equally provides ecological insights relevant to microbiome-informed crop management.

### Beyond the tripartite genome: a widespread, mobile and gene-rich accessory plasmidome expands the genomic landscape of the model symbiont Sinorhizobium meliloti

Sinorhizobium meliloti is one of the best-studied nitrogen-fixing symbionts, yet research has focused almost entirely on its chromosome and two megaplasmids, pSymA and pSymB. Many strains carry additional accessory plasmids, but these large, pSymA-like elements are rarely recovered from short-read assemblies, leaving a hidden part of the genome unexplored. By sequencing 346 strains with long reads, we recovered 386 accessory plasmids from 76% of strains, with 318 absent from public databases, showing that accessory plasmids are the rule and not the exception. These plasmids were large (34-749 kb), including the largest accessory plasmid reported in S. meliloti, and most were mobilizable or conjugative (relaxases in 85% and mating-pair formation systems in 75% of plasmid clusters), equipping them to move between strains. RepA typing defined 13 replicon groups unique to Sinorhizobium, which could serve as a framework for tracking these plasmids in natural populations. We also found evidence of plasmid expansion by fusion, and one ~315 kb plasmid was a cointegrate of two smaller plasmids from our collection. About two-thirds of plasmid gene families were absent from the main replicons, including defense systems, the anti-restriction protein ArdC, and biosynthetic gene clusters, 53% of which had never been observed before. Overturning the view that S. meliloti lacks restriction-modification systems, we found them in 153 genomes, including on 32 accessory plasmids. Most inferred gene transfers stayed within the family Rhizobiaceae. Accessory plasmids thus form a large, mobile and largely unexplored gene pool that may shape how this model symbiont competes and adapts.
