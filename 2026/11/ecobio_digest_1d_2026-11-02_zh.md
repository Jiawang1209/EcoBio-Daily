## 森林土壤微生物

### Drought and simulated pathogen attack shape ectomycorrhizal communities and soil nutrient dynamics in semi-mature sessile oak

Symbiotic ectomycorrhizal fungi (EMF) are central to oak tree nutrition and stress tolerance, contributing to nutrient acquisition in exchange for host-derived photosynthates. Trees increasingly experience interacting abiotic and biotic stress events, such as drought and pest or pathogen attack, which may reduce C reserves, disrupt physiological functioning, and contribute to a progressive decline in tree vitality and resilience. As part of novel field manipulation experiment on semi-mature oak ( Quercus petraea (Matt.) Liebl.), we studied the effects of severe drought, simulated by installing rain exclusion shelters, and pathogen attack, simulated by partial ringbarking, on soil fungal communities. Altered community composition with increased relative presence of saprotrophic fungi compared with untreated controls was seen in both treatments but EMF communities were more strongly affected by 20 months of simulated drought than by partial ringbarking. The reduced number of EMF species under drought were likely associated with a combination of low soil moisture and drought-induced changes in soil nutrient dynamics, including increased inorganic nitrogen availability. Partial ringbarking did not affect overall EMF community richness compared to the control, but a specific nutrient stress indicator species, Cortinarius sp., was detected. These patterns suggest that reduced belowground carbon allocation and impaired root function under ringbarking may have altered host nutrient demand. This may have favoured the maintenance of nutrient-acquisitive ectomycorrhizal symbionts despite their relatively high carbon requirements. Understanding how EMF communities respond to prolonged environmental stress will be increasingly important for predicting woodland resilience and the progression of oak decline under climate change.

### Maize–potato intercropping enhances aggregate stability and AMF diversity in red soils of Southern China

Abstract Enhancing soil aggregate stability is fundamental to sustaining ecosystem services and mitigating land degradation. However, the mechanisms governing stabilization, specifically the mediatory role of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) in maize ( Zea mays L.)–potato ( Solanum tuberosum L.) intercropping, remain largely uncharacterized. A 5‐year field experiment was conducted to systematically analyze the changes in physicochemical properties, glomalin‐related soil proteins, enzymatic activities, and aggregate stability of non‐rhizosphere soil under different cropping patterns (monoculture and intercropping) and different growth stages (T1, jointing/seedling; T2, tasseling/peak flowering; T3, maturity). Additionally, the AMF community characteristics in rhizosphere and non‐rhizosphere soils were analyzed at the T2 stage. Results showed that the vigorous vegetative growth stage (T2) of both maize and potato exhibited peak extracellular enzyme activities, total glomalin‐related soil protein (T‐GRSP) accumulation, and aggregate stability. Compared with monoculture, maize–potato intercropping significantly enhanced rhizosphere soil urease, invertase, and catalase activities, as well as non‐rhizosphere soil organic matter and easily extractable glomalin‐related soil protein contents at the T2 stage, while concurrently increasing the proportion of >2.0 mm aggregates and mean weight diameter in non‐rhizosphere soil by 10.98%–88.84%. At this stage, potato intercropping increased AMF community diversity while concurrently elevating the relative abundances of Unclassified_f__Gigasporaceae , Scutellospora , and Gigaspora . Furthermore, AMF indirectly enhanced soil aggregate stability via the positive regulation of T‐GRSP. This study clarifies biophysicochemical pathways of crop diversity enhancing soil structural stability, supporting sustainable cropping management and erosion control in diverse cropping systems.

### Management rather than climate and biodiversity shapes long-term delta15N dynamics in grassland soils

The bulk soil δ 15 N signature in grassland soils serves as a fingerprint of nitrogen inputs, turnover and losses, reflecting the influence of land management, climate, plant biodiversity and abiotic soil properties.Although natural abundance δ 15 N has been proposed as an indicator for these factors, previous studies yielded mixed results regarding the importance of its drivers.In this study, we quantified changes in soil δ 15 N across 72 German grassland sites after 12 years (2011 vs. 2023) and analysed relationships with detailed records of land use intensity, plant biodiversity, climate, and soil properties.Land use intensity was quantified on a continuous scale based on mowing, grazing and fertilisation.In the top 10 cm of soil, δ 15 N changes ranged from -0.86 to + 2.49‰, equating to an average increase of + 0.35‰ per decade.Over two-thirds of our study sites showed a long-term increase in δ 15 N associated with high land use intensity.Our findings indicate that fertiliser application and grazing are the dominant drivers of δ 15 N increases, while a biodiversity-mediated decrease via reduced N losses was not observed.The slow decadal-scale shifts suggest that single soil δ 15 N measurements without known initial values are only informative for long-term management processes and not for assessing recent management impacts.
