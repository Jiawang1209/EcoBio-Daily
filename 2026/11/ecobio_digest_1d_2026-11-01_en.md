### BIOLOGICAL NITROGEN FIXATION: MECHANISM, MICROORGANISMS AND ITS ROLE IN SUSTAINABLE AGRICULTURE

Nitrogen is one of the most essential elements required for the growth, development and survival of living organisms. It plays an important role in the formation of amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, enzymes and several other biologically important compounds. Although atmospheric nitrogen is abundant in the form of molecular nitrogen (N₂), most plants are unable to use it directly because the two nitrogen atoms are strongly linked by a stable triple covalent bond. Nitrogen -fixation is therefore an important natural and biological process that converts atmospheric nitrogen into forms that can enter biological systems and support plant growth. Free-living organisms such as Azotobacter, Rhizobium and Clostridium etc. can contribute to nitrogen availability without forming a direct symbiotic relationship with plants. Associative nitrogen fixers occur in close proximity to plant roots and benefit from compounds released by the plant. The interaction between Rhizobium and legumes involves several stages, including recognition of plant signals, production of Nod factors, root-hair responses, formation of infection threads, development of root nodules and differentiation of bacteria into nitrogen-fixing bacteroid. This highly coordinated interaction enables the exchange of nutrients between the plant and microorganism, in which the plant supplies carbon compounds and the bacterial partner contributes fixed nitrogen. Overall, biological nitrogen fixation represents a significant connection between atmospheric nitrogen, microorganisms, plants and soil fertility. A better understanding of nitrogen-fixing organisms, their mechanisms, isolation methods, culture conditions and agricultural applications can support the development of sustainable strategies for improving plant nutrition and maintaining soil health.

### EFFECTS OF CHEMICAL POLLUTANTS ON SOIL MICROBIAL COMMUNITIES: MECHANISMS, ECOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES, AND SUSTAINABLE MANAGEMENT

Soil microorganisms regulate nutrient transformations that sustain plant productivity and ecosystem resilience. Chemical pollutants can disrupt these functions by altering microbial abundance, community composition, enzyme activity, functional gene expression, and interactions among microorganisms. This critical review examines the effects of pesticides, herbicides, heavy metals, antibiotics, petroleum hydrocarbons, and persistent organic pollutants (POPs) on soil microorganisms involved in nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and sulfur (S) cycling. Evidence indicates that pollutant effects are strongly contextdependent, varying with chemical structure, concentration, exposure duration, soil pH, organic matter content, nutrient status, and pre-existing microbial adaptation. Pesticides and herbicides can inhibit potential nitrification, N-acetylglucosaminidase activity, diazotrophic activity, and N-cycle functional genes, although some field studies report transient community shifts or recovery after dissipation. Heavy metals commonly constrain microbial biomass, enzyme activities, and nutrient mineralisation through toxicity, oxidative stress, membrane damage, and selection for resistant taxa. Antibiotics may alter microbial N transformations and promote communities carrying resistance determinants, whereas petroleum hydrocarbons and POPs can suppress sensitive nitrifiers and nutrient-cycling taxa while enriching pollutant-degrading organisms. Disrupted P cycling often manifests as altered phosphatase activity, phosphate-solubilising populations, and microbial P immobilisation; effects on S cycling, including arylsulfatase activity and sulfate reduction or oxidation, remain comparatively underexamined. These changes may reduce nutrient availability, destabilise soil organic matter turnover, impair plant growth, increase N losses, and compromise sustainable agriculture. Sustainable management should prioritise pollution prevention, integrated pest management, contaminant-specific risk assessment, organic amendments, biochar, phytoremediation, and microbiome-informed bioremediation. Future research should combine long-term field experiments with multi-omics, direct rate measurements, and assessments of pollutant mixtures to distinguish temporary compositional changes from persistent loss of soil function.

### Variation in rhizosphere microbial communities and nitrogen- and phosphorus-cycling functional genes among rubber plantations in Yunnan, China

Introduction Rhizosphere microorganisms are important participants in soil nutrient transformation. However, variation in rhizosphere microbial community composition and nitrogen (N)- and phosphorus (P)-cycling functional gene profiles among rubber plantations in Yunnan Province remains unclear. Methods Metagenomic sequencing was used to compare rhizosphere microbial community composition and N- and P-cycling functional gene profiles among the rubber plantations located in five major natural rubber-producing areas in Yunnan Province, China: Gengma (MD), Ximeng (XM), Mengla (ML), Jinghong (JH), and Jiangcheng (JC). Results Bacterial and fungal community composition and N- and P-cycling functional gene profiles differed significantly among the five rubber plantations. JC exhibited higher fungal diversity and richness, but its bacterial diversity was significantly lower than that in MD. The bacterial communities were dominated by Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, and Actinomycetota, and the relative abundance of Acidobacteria was significantly enriched in JC compared to JH. Furthermore, Mucoromycota, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota were the dominant phyla in the fungal community. At the functional level, compared to JC, key genes associated with N cycling were significantly enriched in ML, including N assimilation ( glnA , gltB , gltD ), dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium ( narG , narH , nirB ), nitrification ( nxrA , nxrB ), and denitrification ( napA , napB ). Furthermore, the nosZ gene was also significantly enriched in MD compared to JC. Regarding the P cycle, compared to JC, genes associated with high-affinity phosphate transporters ( pstA , pstB , pstC ) and polyphosphate degradation ( ndk ) were significantly enriched in MD, whereas the organic phosphorus mineralization-related gene phnX exhibited the opposite trend. Meanwhile, compared to MD, the polyphosphate degradation-related gene ppk2 was significantly enriched in ML, whereas the inorganic phosphorus solubilization-related gene pqqC exhibited the opposite pattern. Hierarchical partitioning identified soil pH, total phosphorus (TP), and total potassium (TK) as the primary factors significantly associated with variations in bacterial community composition and P-cycling functional gene profiles. Conclusion In summary, the composition of rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities, as well as the functional gene profiles related to N and P cycling, differed significantly among the investigated rubber plantations. These variations were closely associated with soil physicochemical factors. These findings may provide a microbiological basis for further developing site-specific nutrient management strategies in rubber plantations.

### 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of rhizosphere bacterial communities in rice lines carrying nitrogen-use-related alleles

This project investigates rhizosphere bacterial communities associated with nitrogen-use-related alleles and their combinations in rice (Oryza sativa). The plant materials comprise the recurrent parent HJX74, four single-segment substitution lines carrying IAPAR9-derived alleles associated with OsWRKY23, DNR1, RNR10 and OsWRI1a, and five double-segment pyramiding lines. Plants were grown under no nitrogen addition (LN) and nitrogen addition (HN) conditions. A total of 60 rhizosphere soil samples were collected. Three separately collected pooled rhizosphere soil samples were obtained per genotype under each nitrogen condition, each comprising rhizosphere soil from five seedlings. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F/806R and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform with paired-end 300-bp reads. These data support comparisons of rhizosphere bacterial community diversity and composition among rice materials and nitrogen conditions, together with the identification of differentially abundant ASVs and candidates with predicted nitrogen-cycle-related functions.

### Compost from urban biowaste shapes the bacterial community and its functional traits in the rhizosphere of tomato plants

Industrial compost derived from urban biowaste is a sustainable biofertilizer made from food waste and plant matter. It has long been used as a fertilizer in agriculture and horticulture for its rich nutrient content. However, the composition of the microbiota in industrial biowaste compost has been studied only partially, and the effects of this bacterial community on soil, rhizosphere, and plant root communities have received limited attention. In this study, we conducted community and functional analyses to examine how the microbiota in biowaste compost influences bacterial diversity, community composition, and metabolic potential in tomato plants, compared with plants fertilized with conventional, unsustainable chemical fertilizer. Source contribution analysis tracking bacterial taxa indicated a significant contribution of compost-derived taxa to the soil, rhizosphere, and roots. The community analysis revealed distinct clustering by fertilizer type, with biowaste compost promoting a unique, more diverse rhizosphere community, enriched in Firmicutes species. Functional analysis highlighted increased microbial pathways in the rhizosphere treated with biowaste compost, including enzymes for cellulolytic and xylanolytic activity, nitrogenase, phytase, and 1-ACC deaminase. Overall, food-derived industrial biowaste compost enhances bacterial diversity and the predicted functional and plant growth-promoting potential in the rhizosphere of tomato plants, underscoring its potential as a sustainable biofertilizer that supports soil health, microbial biodiversity, and crop productivity.

### Long-term Effects of Partially Substituting Chemical Fertilizers with Different Organic Amendments

The long-term effects of different organic soil amendments remain unclear. In this study, a 9-year field experiment was conducted in Shandong Province, China, to evaluate the impacts of different organic amendments on apple yield, soil physicochemical properties, and microbial community structure. The results indicated that partial substitution of chemical fertilizers with bio-organic amendments mitigated soil acidification, increased soil organic matter content and nutrient content, enhanced the proportion of water-stable aggregates, improved overall soil physicochemical properties, and optimized soil microbial community structure. Redundancy and correlation analyses revealed layer-specific relationships. At the 0–20 cm soil layer, the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota was significantly positively correlated with soil electrical conductivity (EC). In contrast, the relative abundances of Gemmatimonadota and Pseudomonadota were both significantly negatively correlated with soil EC. At the 20–40 cm layer, the relative abundances of Acidobacteriota , Basidiomycota , and Ascomycota were significantly positively correlated with the content of water-stable large aggregates. These results hold significant theoretical implications for the research and promotion of various types of organic amendments.

### Influence of Gliricidia sepium living support on rhizosphere bacterial recruitment and fruit metabolomics in black pepper (Piper nigrum)

Abstract The ability of the rhizosphere to recruit beneficial microorganisms is central to the sustainable management of crops. Here, we investigated, through shotgun metagenomic sequencing and mass spectrometry techniques, the microbial composition, antimicrobial resistance (AMRs), and metabolomic profiles of Piper nigrum cultivated under two support systems: living Gliricidia sepium support (SL/RL) and dead wooden support (SD/RD). Bulk and rhizosphere soils were sequenced to assess taxonomic structure and ARGs, with emphasis on Actinobacteria due to their relevance in antibiotic production. Actinobacteria were highly represented across the samples (~ 35%). Dead-support rhizospheres exhibited a broader ARG repertoire and greater abundance of taxa known to include antibiotic-producing members, whereas living-support rhizospheres showed reduced ARG diversity. Metabolomic analysis further revealed support-dependent physiological responses in the host plant. PLS-DA distinguished the two cultivation systems, and three defense-related flavonoids, such as sakuranetin, isovitexin, and orientin, which were enriched in fruits of plants grown with dead support, indicating activation of stress – and defense – associated metabolic pathways. Their low abundance in living-support plants suggests reduced physiological stress, likely linked to improved nutrient cycling and rhizosphere stability provided by G. sepium . However, the volatile and total phenolic profile did not display significant chemical alterations in the samples. Together, these results suggest that support type influences both microbial functions and plant secondary metabolism. Dead support creates a more stressful ecological context that enriches antimicrobial resistance genes ARGs and defense metabolites, whereas living support promotes a more balanced soil-plant-microbe environment, supporting the use of G. sepium as a sustainable and biologically advantageous cultivation strategy for black pepper.

### Management rather than climate and biodiversity shapes long-term delta15N dynamics in grassland soils

The bulk soil δ 15 N signature in grassland soils serves as a fingerprint of nitrogen inputs, turnover and losses, reflecting the influence of land management, climate, plant biodiversity and abiotic soil properties.Although natural abundance δ 15 N has been proposed as an indicator for these factors, previous studies yielded mixed results regarding the importance of its drivers.In this study, we quantified changes in soil δ 15 N across 72 German grassland sites after 12 years (2011 vs. 2023) and analysed relationships with detailed records of land use intensity, plant biodiversity, climate, and soil properties.Land use intensity was quantified on a continuous scale based on mowing, grazing and fertilisation.In the top 10 cm of soil, δ 15 N changes ranged from -0.86 to + 2.49‰, equating to an average increase of + 0.35‰ per decade.Over two-thirds of our study sites showed a long-term increase in δ 15 N associated with high land use intensity.Our findings indicate that fertiliser application and grazing are the dominant drivers of δ 15 N increases, while a biodiversity-mediated decrease via reduced N losses was not observed.The slow decadal-scale shifts suggest that single soil δ 15 N measurements without known initial values are only informative for long-term management processes and not for assessing recent management impacts.
