### A national baseline for methane sink habitats and methanotroph diversity

Methane emissions account for nearly a third of the Earth’s effective radiative forcing, with methanotrophs playing a critical role in mitigating emissions by oxidising methane in diverse environments. Despite their ecological importance, methanotrophic diversity and environmental distribution remain incompletely characterised due to cultivation challenges, incomplete or low-quality metagenome-assembled genomes, and limited taxonomic resolution in marker gene surveys. Here, we present a national study of the biogeography of novel and known methanotrophs across Denmark’s major natural, urban and agricultural habitats, using genome-resolved classification of 10,683 metagenomes and 102 new methanotrophic species. By linking metabolic potential to habitat-specific distributions, we reveal uncharacterised methanotrophs as dominant in natural ecosystems. These findings provide a comprehensive baseline of methanotroph diversity, reveal clear contrasts between natural and disturbed habitats, and highlight candidate species and habitats for future methane-mitigation strategies. In this study, the authors use metagenomics to map the diverse and previously unknown bacteria responsible for taking up methane across Denmark’s varied environments and highlight the natural habitats and methanotroph species with the potential for methane mitigation.

### Anthropogenic acidification decouples nitrogen cycling processes and compromises soil nitrogen retention

Soil acidification threatens agricultural sustainability, yet its consequences for nitrogen (N) cycling remain unclear. By comparing N-cycling responses across N addition, acid addition and natural pH gradients along a common acidification dimension, we disentangle acidification effects from confounding factors. Despite comparable pH declines, the three regimes of acidification elicited divergent responses in N cycling. Acid addition uniformly suppressed N cycling by 15.6–39.9%, whereas natural pH gradients has little effect. Notably, N-addition-induced acidification uncoupled N cycling, enhancing mineralization by 29.7% while reducing immobilization, nitrification, and denitrification by 6.8%, 20.6% and 17.3%, respectively. These responses intensified with acidification severity, tracked changes in microbial functional genes and enzyme activities, and depleted soil total N and dissolved organic N by 28.8% and 60.5%, respectively. Our findings identify acidification as a key pathway through which intensive N fertilization erodes soil fertility, highlighting the need to integrate acidification remediation into sustainable nutrient management.

### Valley-bottom wetland agricultural conversion and recovery shape greenhouse gas dynamics and soil organic carbon stocks in an African tropical highland system

Tropical wetlands significantly impact global greenhouse gas (GHG) budgets and carbon storage; however, data from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remain limited. Highland valley-bottom wetland (HVBW) agriculture supports millions, yet its effects on GHG emissions and carbon storage remain undocumented. This study quantified soil emissions of nitrous oxide (N 2 O), carbon dioxide (CO 2 ), and methane (CH 4 ) in the Taita Hills, Kenya, from 12 converted, 10 recovering, and one reference (intact) HVBW. Agricultural conversion shifted wetland emissions from CH 4 to N 2 O dominance. Converted HVBWs were N 2 O sources (1.6 kgN2O-Nha-1yr-1), driven by elevated soil nitrate, whereas the intact HVBW was an N 2 O sink (−0.3 kgN2O-Nha-1yr-1), with high soil moisture and a high soil C/NO3--N ratio suggesting complete denitrification. Recovering HVBWs showed intermediate N 2 O emissions (0.5 kgN2O-Nha-1yr-1). CO 2 emissions were similar between converted and recovering HVBWs (9534 vs. 9610 kgCO2-Cha-1yr-1) but lower in the intact (2923 kgCO2-Cha-1yr-1). CH 4 emissions were highest in the intact HVBW (2757 kgCH4-Cha-1yr-1), intermediate in recovering sites (781 kgCH4-Cha-1yr-1), and lowest in converted sites (38 kgCH4-Cha-1yr-1). The intact HVBW had an SOC stock of 224 Mg C ha −1 , indicating carbon loss rates of 2.7 MgCha-1yr-1 over 45 years for converted HVBWs. Comparing recovering with converted HVBWs suggests an SOC recovery of about 1.1 MgCha-1yr-1 after abandonment, offsetting ∼0.0005 % of Kenya's annual agricultural GHG emissions, or 9.8 % when scaled nationally. However, recovering HVBWs also emitted considerably more CH 4 , so their net climate effect cannot be derived from SOC stocks alone. These findings highlight trade-offs between GHG emissions and carbon storage in HVBWs, with wetland recovery promoting partial functional restoration and SOC recovery in SSA, but at the cost of increased CH 4 emissions.

### Plant-soil interactions drive GHG dynamics in organic soil under variable water tables: a case study with poplar

Organic soils provide a substantial capacity for carbon storage both in below- and above-ground biomass, but they are also a significant contributor to natural terrestrial greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Management of soils with high organic matter content, particularly through drainage and productivity enhancement, often leads to increased CO 2 emissions. By monitoring in a controlled environment, it is possible to determine how groundwater level and environmental conditions influence the carbon cycle, including plant vitality and productivity, and consequently shape future carbon sequestration potential. The aim of this study was to develop a system under semi-controlled conditions to assess the impact of different groundwater levels on GHG emissions, accumulated biomass, and tree vitality. We determined the effects of different groundwater levels: −2; −15; −25; and −35 cm. We monitored CH 4 and CO 2 autotrophic and heterotrophic emission, soil chemical analyses, and plant morphological (biomass, root and leaf area, shoot length) and physiological (leaf chlorophyll a and b content indices) parameters. Temporal variation strongly impacted GHG fluxes, while diurnal variation in CO 2 emissions depended on groundwater level, with a distinct day–night response at −35 cm, whereas no groundwater-dependent diurnal response was detected for CH 4 . During soil temperature extremes, extremely high CH 4 emissions occurred at a −2 cm groundwater level. Higher plant productivity had a greater influence on GHG fluxes: it decreased both CH 4 and CO 2 emissions during the day compared to bare soil. Therefore, the autotrophic respiration rate increased with increased productivity, but the primary determinant was heterotrophic respiration. The study demonstrates that groundwater level, together with plant productivity and environmental conditions, jointly regulates GHG emissions in organic soil.
