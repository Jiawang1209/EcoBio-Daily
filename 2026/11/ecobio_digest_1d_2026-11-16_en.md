### Plastic matters: it drives soil aggregation, carbon retention, nutrient cycling, and microbial communities dependent on its degradability

Microplastics (MPs) have shown different effects on soil physical structure, chemistry, and microbial communities. As soils are known to differ in their potential to develop structure, the observed variability in MP effects may be strongly attributed to MPs impact on soil aggregation. Hence, we here address the impact of different MPs on soil aggregation and associated soil functions such as water, carbon, and nutrient storage, and microbial habitat in soils differing in texture and soil organic matter (SOM). We hypothesized that conventional MPs hinder soil aggregation, speeding up the turnover of soil inherent OM, and reducing soil water holding capacity (WHC), while degradable MPs represent substrates altering soil microbial community composition, and directly promoting microbial activity and nutrient turnover. To address this, Zea mays was planted in a greenhouse with silty loam and sandy loam soil and spiked with polyethylene (PE), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), or biodegradable polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), varied by concentration (0%, 0.1%, 1% w/w) and size (200–400, 75–200, <75 µm). Soil aggregate fractions and stability, WHC, 15 N-ammonium-nitrate and 13 C-yeast as models for soil N and C cycling, available P and K, as well as plant growth, and microbiota within aggregate fractions were analyzed. Overall, MP addition led to a decrease in more stable, occluded soil microaggregates, and a respective increase in free aggregates, while macroaggregate fractions remained unaffected. For both soils and across all MPs, soil C retention was reduced about 50%. Soil texture dominated the response of soil to MP addition: finer-textured soils lost more microaggregates, around 25% greater SOM turnover, and 33% lower WHC. As this was independent of polymer size and type, we relate this to physical MP effects. An MP-specific biochemical effect was observed for biodegradable PBAT tending to increase soil microaggregate formation, stability, and WHC. It led to 15 N immobilization in soil and increased plant uptake and significantly altered soil prokaryotic communities in aggregate fractions. Here, microbial taxa involved in reductive nitrogen cycling, depletion of N-fixing Azospirillum spp., and enrichment of the potentially plant-pathogenic Xylophilus spp. was observed. We reveal that MPs exert fundamentally bidirectional effects on soils. Biodegradable MPs enhance beneficial properties such as aggregation and water retention yet concurrently disrupt key microbiome functions. Across all polymer types, MPs accelerate soil C turnover, underscoring the need to resolve their net impact on critical soil functions in a soil- and polymer-specific context.

### An exploratory evidence map of mechanisms, field evidence, and research gaps in plant-soil microbiome interactions for sustainable agriculture based exclusively on PubMed-indexed records

Soil microorganisms mediate nutrient turnover, carbon storage, disease suppression, and plant stress tolerance, yet the evidentiary distance between microbial measurements and agronomic outcomes remains poorly defined. An evidence map is needed that states exactly what has been reported, in which study settings, and with what reporting gaps. An exploratory evidence map of PubMed-indexed records published from 1 January 2015 to 29 September 2026 was conducted. A Boolean query combining soil, rhizosphere, and plant microbiome concepts with crop and agricultural terms was executed on 29 September 2026 through the NCBI E-utilities. Records were deduplicated by identifier and normalised title, screened with prespecified automated title/abstract rules, and classified with lexical tags for evidence domain, study design, geography, intervention class, and crop type. Tag accuracy was quantified on 194 manually annotated records drawn from seven strata, and precision, recall, and 95% Wilson confidence intervals were computed. Of 11,061 records identified, 4 duplicates and 573 records excluded at screening left 10,484 screen-positive records. Tags indicated microbial inoculants (6,695; 63.9%), climate or environmental stress (2,748; 26.2%), nutrient cycling (2,453; 23.4%), and plant health or disease (2,377; 22.7%). An explicit field design was stated in 1,256 records (12.0%), with a validation-adjusted field share of 22.1% (95% CI 13.1%—37.5%). Economic outcomes appeared in 593 records (5.7%) and negative or null result signals in 392 (3.7%). Manual validation gave precision of 93.3% for eligibility, 90.0% for the inoculant tag, 84.0% for the stress tag, and 100% for geographic tagging. Within the PubMed-indexed literature, field verification, economic outcomes, and negative or null results are under-represented relative to controlled-environment and molecular studies. The map defines a PubMed-specific retrieval landscape, and the quantified gaps specify what a subsequent multi-database, study-level systematic review should verify.

### Spatiotemporal patterns of bacterial and fungal communities in rhizosphere and root endosphere of endangered Taihangia rupestris var. ciliata and their edaphic drivers

Taihangia rupestris var. ciliata is a nationally protected endangered plant classified as a Class II species. To date, no studies have investigated its associated microbial communities. This study aimed to characterize the diversity, seasonal dynamics, and key drivers of both rhizosphere soil and root endophytic microbial communities, with the goal of providing microbiological insights to support the artificial propagation and conservation of this endangered species. The results showed that the habitat soil of T. rupestris var. ciliata was slightly alkaline. Soil pH, electrical conductivity, organic matter, and hydrolyzable nitrogen content peaked in June, whereas available phosphorus reached its maximum in October. The alpha diversity of rhizosphere microorganisms was significantly higher than that of root endophytic communities, indicating clear niche differentiation between these two compartments. Rhizosphere bacteria were predominantly composed of Acidobacteriota, Chloroflexota, and ​Actinomycetota, while endophytic bacteria were mainly represented by​ Pseudomonadota and ​Cyanobacteriota. Ascomycota was the absolute dominant phylum across all fungal communities. Rhizosphere bacteria exhibited active carbon and nitrogen cycling functions, whereas fungal communities were primarily characterized by saprotrophic and mixed trophic modes. Regarding community assembly, rhizosphere bacterial communities were mainly governed by neutral processes, while fungal communities were shaped by deterministic processes. Source tracking analysis revealed that over 92.12% of rhizosphere bacteria originated from unknown habitats, with no significant seasonal variation. In contrast, root endophytic microorganisms were primarily derived from local rhizosphere soil and were more sensitive to seasonal changes. Organic matter, electrical conductivity, and hydrolyzable nitrogen were important soil physicochemical factors affecting community composition. In conclusion, significant niche differentiation exists between the rhizosphere and root endophytic microbial communities of T. rupestris var. ciliata . Their seasonal dynamics are jointly regulated by host phenology and soil physicochemical properties, and the unique microbial community characteristics may represent an important survival strategy for this species to adapt to barren rocky habitats.

### Uncoupling traits, colonization, and benefits: genotype-dependent growth promotion by endophytic bacteria in Poaceae, including perennial Kernza

Abstract Thinopyrum intermedium (Kernza) is a perennial Poaceae crop with potential benefits for soil health and contributes to climate change mitigation. In this study, we investigated whether bacterial functional traits and abundance predict growth responses across different Poaceae species. From 21 investigated bacterial strains representing endophytic and rhizosphere strains, five endophytes ( Labedella endophytica FR-P-L-64, Pseudomonas lini FR-P-R2-32, Pantoea sp. GT2-2-ED1, Pseudomonas stutzeri ISE-12, Streptomyces phaeochromogenes ISE-4) were selected based on their plant-growth promoting (PGP) traits related to phytohormone production, nutrient assimilation, and stress alleviation. Phenotypic profiling revealed substantial metabolic diversity among the selected strains: P. lini FR-P-R2-32 showed the broadest spectrum of substrate utilization, while S. phaeochromogenes ISE-4 utilized only 10 of the 94 substrates. The strains were evaluated for their ability to promote plant growth in different Poaceae hosts, including two wheat cultivars, two perennial ryegrass varieties, and the perennial grain crop Kernza. Plant responses to inoculation were genotype-dependent: only S. phaeochromogenes ISE-4 increased shoot and root dry weight in Kernza and wheat cv. Euforia, whereas the remaining strains produced neutral or negative effects. Strain-specific quantitative PCR targeting the 16 S rRNA gene revealed distinct colonization patterns in shoots and roots including systemic, organ-specific, and limited colonization. Regression models indicated that post-inoculation endophyte abundance was positively or negatively associated with plant biomass, depending on the plant genotype. These findings highlight​ the importance of host genotype in determining the effectiveness of bacterial bioinoculants. This study provides the first functional characterization of bacterial endophytes isolated from T . intermedium.

### From disease to suppressiveness: an epistemological perspective on Fusarium wilt

Banana Fusarium wilt provides a compelling case for reconsidering the explanatory scope of contemporary plant pathology. Although Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc) is the etiological agent of the disease, pathogen presence alone cannot explain the substantial variation in disease expression observed across soils, host genotypes, and production environments. Increasing evidence indicates that suppressiveness emerges from interactions among pathogen pressure, host traits, rhizosphere microbiomes, nutrient availability, soil physicochemical properties, and agricultural management. This critical narrative review integrates recent findings on banana Fusarium wilt with concepts from philosophy of science and environmental ethics. We argue that suppressiveness should be understood as an emergent property of the soil–plant–microbiome system rather than as an intrinsic attribute of individual antagonistic microorganisms. This interpretation does not replace etiological causation but expands phytopathological explanation toward multilevel ecological causality. Kuhn's account of scientific change and Feyerabend's methodological pluralism provide complementary frameworks for interpreting this expansion, whereas Leopold's land ethic and Jonas's principle of responsibility illuminate its ecological and temporal implications. We further distinguish pathogen reduction, disease control, induced suppressiveness, and phytosanitary resilience as different levels of phytosanitary outcome. A soil-health-oriented plant pathology should therefore integrate pathogen biology, host resistance, soil chemistry, microbial functions, and management while explicitly considering the persistence of disease-regulating processes through time.
