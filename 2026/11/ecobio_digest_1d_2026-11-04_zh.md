### Microbial carbon use efficiency emerges from interactions between soil structure and life-history strategies

Abstract. Microbial carbon use efficiency (CUE) is believed to be a key regulator of the accumulation of persistent soil organic carbon. Microbial processing of organic carbon in soil occurs in the pore network, the properties of which are known to affect microbial activity. However, the effects of interactions between pore architecture, and microbial life-history strategies on carbon dynamics, and house these interactions are modulated by moisture regime, remain poorly understood. Here, we use a spatially explicit model based on a cellular automaton, applied to realistic soil pore networks derived from X-ray tomographic images, to investigate how pore geometry and organic matter distribution shape microbial dynamics and CUE. The model explicitly represents r- and K-strategists that are either motile or immobile, and have different resource requirements for growth, maintenance and motility. The constraints imposed on substrate diffusion and the spatial domain in which motile bacteria can effectively disperse by the moisture regime are explicitly represented in the model, in order to capture how moisture regulate decomposer access to resources. The simulations show that pore architecture has a significant effect on microbial CUE. Micropores promoted high CUE and a dominance of K-strategists by limiting substrate diffusion, reducing pore connectivity, and constraining microbial access to spatially isolated resources. In contrast, macropores with high connectivity and greater substrate accessibility favor r-strategists, rapid biomass turnover and lower CUE. Motility emerges as advantageous in connected, carbon-rich macropores, but energetically unfavourable in confined or poorly connected micropores, where immobile strategies outperform motile ones. Overall, our results indicate that microbial CUE is not an intrinsic property of life-strategies alone, but emerges from the interaction between microbial life-strategies and pore-scale physical constraints. This study suggests that soil structure influences microbial strategies by limiting resource availability and dispersal.

### Peatland climate-methane feedbacks in warming periods were governed by pH and hydrology

Abstract Methane is emitted naturally from peatlands, but controls on those emissions and their response to global warming remain debated. Previous research attempted to assess this by exploring how methane cycling responded to past warming events, with lipid biomarker signatures for methanotrophy (the 13 C-depletion of bacterially-derived hopanoids, δ 13 C hop ) revealing changes to the methane cycle during the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM). However, the lack of modern analogues has hindered interpretation of those changes. We present new δ 13 C hop data from a suite of tropical peatlands, including the discovery of exceptionally 13 C-depleted hopanoids, with values approaching those of the PETM. Across all sites, δ 13 C hop decreases with increasing pH, likely co-occurring with a shift from predominantly hydrogenotrophic to acetoclastic methanogenesis pathways. Modern sites with the lowest δ 13 C hop have elevated methane emissions, higher Type I to Type II methanotroph ratios, and higher methanogen proportions, linking hopanoid isotopic depletion to enhanced methane production and flux. These insights allow re-evaluation of geological records, with low PETM δ 13 C hop also indicating intensified peatland methane emissions. Such processes evidently acted as positive feedbacks in the past and could do so in the future – not only as a direct response to warming but indirectly via changes to peatland hydrology and geochemistry.

### Tandem repeats in methanogenic archaea and their viruses reveal a shared potential for rapid genomic diversification

Methanogenic archaea drive global methane emissions, yet the molecular basis of their physiological flexibility is unclear. Archaeal adaptation is typically attributed to horizontal gene transfer, which is episodic and cannot explain rapid, reversible change. Here we show that direct tandem repeats (TRs) offer an intrinsic alternative. By screening 50,567 prokaryotic genomes, we find TR densities in Methanosarcina more than 30-fold above the prokaryotic average, paralleling Borg extrachromosomal elements. These TRs recur at conserved loci while sharing almost no sequence identity between strains, indicating independent origin, and evolve during routine cultivation. Methanogen TRs are transcribed, show ribosome occupancy, and lie near regulatory and cofactor-related genes, whilst being depleted around core energy-conserving and translational machinery. Genic TRs generate intrinsically disordered regions in proteins carrying nucleotide-binding, protein-protein interaction and cell-surface domains, and occur in structural and surface proteins of methanogen-infecting viruses. TRs are therefore an overlooked source of variability and adaptation potential in methanogens and their viruses.

### Comparative Analysis of Rhizosphere Microbiome Diversity in Lentil Landraces and Bred Cultivars

Lentil (Lens culinaris Medik.) is a major grain legume of Mediterranean agriculture and global diets, valued for its nutritional and agronomic importance. Although its symbiosis with rhizobia has been extensively studied, considerably less is known about the broader rhizosphere microbial communities associated with genetically diverse lentil germplasm. In this study, targeted amplicon sequencing was used to characterize the rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities associated with a diverse collection of lentil landraces and commercial cultivars grown simultaneously under identical field conditions. The integration of SSR-based genetic characterization with microbial community profiling enabled a complementary assessment of the association between host genetic diversity and rhizosphere microbial community composition. A conserved core microbiome was identified across all genotypes, while distinct differences in microbial diversity and community composition were observed among individual genotypes, indicating distinct genotype-associated microbial community profiles. Differential associations of several microbial taxa, including diazotrophic, plant growth-promoting, and potential soil-borne pathogenic microorganisms, were also observed among genotypes despite their cultivation under identical environmental conditions. Collectively, this study provides a comparative characterization of rhizosphere microbial communities associated with a genetically diverse lentil germplasm collection and establishes a genetically characterized framework for future comparative studies of plant-microbe interactions. These findings open new perspectives for breeding strategies that consider beneficial plant-microbe associations alongside host genetic diversity.

### Crop-adapted arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in maize: from symbiotic function to field reliability

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) can improve nutrient acquisition and stress responses in maize, yet inoculation benefits remain inconsistent across fields. Root colonization alone does not establish effective nutrient transfer or net crop benefit. This critical narrative review examines maize-associated AMF diversity, arbuscule-mediated exchange, host genotype effects, phosphorus availability, stress responses, rhizosphere and hyphosphere interactions, inoculant quality, and field evidence. We define crop-adapted AMF by demonstrated function in a specified maize–soil–management context, rather than fungal origin or colonization percentage alone. Drawing on mechanistic research and contrasting field outcomes, we propose a six-stage Maize–Soil–AMF Fit Framework linking native resource mapping, functional screening, compatibility tests, formulation quality, field integration, and ecological monitoring. The framework distinguishes practical agronomic evidence from advanced mechanistic measurements and permits three decisions: inoculate with a validated product, manage an effective native AMF network, or avoid intervention. It is a conceptual decision aid, not a validated predictive model. Reliable application requires replicated, genotype-aware field tests with appropriate native-AMF controls, prespecified crop outcomes, and transparent assessment of uncertainty and economic value.

### Exploring the role of soil amendments and agronomic practices in enhancing Gossypium hirsutum L. resilience to climate change: a path forward for sustainable cotton farming

Abstract Cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) is an important fiber crop, but its yield and overall fiber quality are constrained by biotic and abiotic factors. Under certain conditions, moderate rises in temperature and elevated atmospheric CO₂ levels may enhance cotton growth and productivity. However, these benefits are often constrained by heat and water stress. This review critically synthesizes the research on the contribution of soil amendments and agronomic practices to improving soil structure, nutrient retention, rhizosphere functionality, and plant stress resilience in cotton systems. Organic, mineral, and microbial amendments, including biochar formulations, can improve soil properties and microbial activity, especially under arid and saline conditions. Nevertheless, their effectiveness is highly context-dependent. Cotton exhibits physiological plasticity through changes in rooting patterns, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defense mechanisms, but its reproductive system is highly sensitive to heat stress. Phenological evidence suggests that warming accelerates crop development and increases the risk of heat and water stress during sensitive growth stages. Based on this synthesis, we propose the Climate-resilient cotton production model (CR-CPM), which conceptualizes cotton resilience as a threshold-based outcome emerging from interactions among soil, microbial, physiological, phenological, and management processes, where physiological thresholds determine the transition from effective management to system-level stress under extreme conditions. This framework emphasizes the need for integrated, site-specific strategies to sustain cotton productivity and soil health under increasing climatic stress.

### Limited effect of basalt amendment and mycorrhizal inoculation on soil chemical properties and maize growth on a nutrient-rich soil

Abstract Enhanced weathering (EW) of silicate minerals has emerged as a promising carbon dioxide removal (CDR) strategy, with potential benefits for soil fertility and crop performance. However, the soil processes that determine these co-benefits remain poorly constrained. In particular, interactions between basalt amendments and soil biota such as arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) may influence nutrient mobilization and plant uptake, but these effects have rarely been quantified. In a 113-day mesocosm experiment with Zea mays using a Belgian, sandy loam soil, we investigated the effect of basalt and AMF inoculation on soil properties, nutrient and heavy metal availability, and crop yield and quality. We also assessed potential AMF-driven bio-weathering via cation mass balance and pore water dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), pH, and alkalinity measurements. Basalt application, but not AMF, improved soil pH, cation exchange capacity, base saturation, and generally increased exchangeable Ca and Mg, whereas most other nutrients in the pore water remained unaffected. Crop yield and quality were largely unaltered by basalt or AMF, except for an increase in plant Mg with basalt application. Moreover, heavy metal availability and plant uptake were also generally unaffected, with the notable exception of soil pore water and corn Ni, which increased with basalt. These results suggest that risk for heavy metal contamination is not generic but may arise under specific environmental conditions. Finally, despite a synergistic effect of basalt and AMF on pore water DIC, we found no indication that AMF enhanced basalt weathering rates. Overall, AMF had limited influence on soil fertility indicators and crop performance. Basalt application improved key soil chemical indicators and increased the exchangeable fractions of Ca and Mg, demonstrating its role as a soil improver. Unlike several studies conducted in more acidic soils, these chemical enhancements did not increase maize growth here, indicating that the agronomic benefits of basalt are context-dependent.
