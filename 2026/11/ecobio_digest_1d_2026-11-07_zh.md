### Specialist–generalist trade-offs in microbial growth rates across soil habitats

Microbial life strategies are shaped by a fundamental ecological trade-off: specialization enables peak performance in a narrow niche, while generalization allows persistence across diverse environments, but may constrain maximal growth rates. In soils, this trade-off is particularly relevant in the rhizosphere and detritusphere-and at their interface, where co-occurring living roots and decaying residues are hypothesized to generate emergent biogeochemical conditions. Using H₂¹⁸O quantitative stable isotope probing (qSIP), we measured bacterial and fungal growth rates in the rhizosphere, the detritusphere, and in the combined presence of rhizosphere + root detritus. We classified taxa operationally as specialists (defined as microbes actively growing only within a single habitat) or generalists (defined as microbes actively growing in two or more habitats), reflecting realized growth breadth under the conditions tested. Bacterial specialists exhibited faster growth than generalists within the habitats where they grew, a pattern consistent with a trade-off between niche breadth and growth rate. Net relatedness and nearest taxon indices showed that total bacterial communities were phylogenetically clustered, while specialist and generalist communities were phylogenetically random, consistent with functional traits-rather than lineage-predicting realized growth-breadth strategy. In annual grassland soils, fast-growing specialists may dominate ecosystem processes when resources abound, and slow-growing generalists may sustain element transformations when conditions shift; understanding this interplay may inform predictions of soil-carbon trajectories. IMPORTANCE: O -an approach that measures active growth rather than mere presence-we tracked bacterial and fungal growth rates across three root-influenced habitats: the rhizosphere, the detritusphere, and the combined rhizosphere + detritusphere. We found that microbes detected growing in only one habitat ("specialists") tended to grow faster within their habitat than microbes detected growing across multiple habitats ("generalists"), a pattern consistent with a trade-off between niche breadth and growth rate. This pattern was clearer for bacteria than for fungi. Although these classifications describe realized growth breadth under the conditions tested rather than universal organismal traits, understanding where and when specialists or generalists dominate may help inform predictions about the fate of soil carbon under changing plant inputs.

### Development of a Four-Strain Rhizobacterial Consortium: Detection in the Field by Strain-Specific qPCR and Impact on the Wheat Rhizosphere Microbiome

Multi-strain consortia are expected to protect plants more reliably than single strains, yet this is rarely tested directly, and the behavior of introduced strains in the field is difficult to follow. We assembled a consortium of Stenotrophomonas rhizophila MGMM118, Bacillus halotolerans MGMM119, Pseudomonas grimontii MGMM120 and Pseudomonas viciae MGMM121 strains isolated from the winter wheat rhizosphere and characterized their hydrolytic activities, auxin production, and analyzed their genomes. The consortium increased shoot and root length of wheat. In biocontrol experiments on barley, the consortium reduced the root rot disease index caused by Fusarium culmorum by approximately ten-fold (5.6% vs. 59.7%), outperforming each individual strain (11.9–17.9%). The consortium also reduced Fusarium root rot in tomato by two-fold. In a field experiment on wheat, strain-specific qPCR detected all introduced microorganisms in the wheat rhizosphere at up to 106 CFU per gram of soil, demonstrating different dynamics for each individual strain. Metabarcoding showed that community structure depended on plant developmental stage rather than on the consortium application. Since metabarcoding could not reliably identify the introduced strains, we suppose that strain-specific qPCR is a more appropriate tool for monitoring of inoculants, particularly in complex soil microbial communities.

### Nitrogen Leaching and Diversity of Ammonia-Oxidizing Archaea and Bacteria in Rice–Frog Co-Cultivation

Rice–frog co-culture is an organic farming practice known to effectively reduce nitrogen (N) loss from paddy fields. Ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA) and bacteria (AOB) mediate the rate-limiting step of nitrification; however, their relative contributions to this process remain unclear in certain soil types. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of rice–frog co-culture on N leaching and the associated AOA and AOB communities. A two-year field experiment was conducted in Shanghai, comparing three cultivation patterns: conventional rice (CR), green rice–frog (GR) co-cultivation, and organic rice–frog co-cultivation (OR). Nitrogen leaching was monitored, soil properties were analyzed, and the abundance and community composition of AOA and AOB were assessed. Nitrogen leaching losses were significantly lower in GR and OR than in CR, by 14.82% and 28.45%, respectively. Ammonium (NH4+) was the predominant form of leached N, accounting for 58.10%, 56.20%, and 49.02% of the total N in CR, GR, and OR, respectively. Rice–frog co-culture significantly increased amoA gene copy numbers of AOA but suppressed those of AOB. The community structures of both AOA and AOB were strongly influenced by soil properties, including NH4+, soil organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (TN), and pH. Specifically, the AOA community was primarily shaped by NH4+ and SOC, whereas the AOB community was mainly driven by SOC content. These findings indicate that integrated rice–frog co-cultivation systems mitigate nitrogen leaching losses, modify soil physicochemical properties, and shift the abundance and community composition of ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria.

### Soil Health Assessment for Crop-Specific Production: Linking Soil Microbiomes, Pathogen Risk, and Crop Nutritional and Phytochemical Quality

Soil degradation is one of the greatest threats to sustainable agriculture, yet current soil health assessment frameworks largely rely on physicochemical indicators and may insufficiently account for crop-specific biological requirements and microbiome functions. Increasing evidence indicates that different crops require distinct combinations of physical, chemical, and biological soil properties and recruit differentially enriched rhizosphere microbial assemblages that regulate nutrient cycling, disease suppression, plant performance, and nutritional quality. This review critically examines current concepts of soil quality and soil health and highlights the need to shift from generalized toward crop-specific soil health assessment. We synthesize the principal physical, chemical, and biological indicators of soil health, with emphasis on soil microbiomes, beneficial microorganisms, soil-borne pathogens, molecular diagnostic approaches, and representative sampling strategies. Using publicly available FAOSTAT and EPPO datasets as an exploratory European case study, we describe continental crop production patterns and the geographical occurrence of major plant pathogens to illustrate how regional crop production and pathogen records can provide context for crop-specific soil health assessment. We further assess evidence linking soil properties and plant-associated microbiomes with crop nutrient composition and bioactive phytochemical accumulation. Collectively, this review proposes a crop-specific conceptual framework that integrates ecological, biological, and agronomic processes to support the interpretation of soil health measurements and provides a basis for future empirical validation.
