## 森林土壤微生物

### Microbial communities do not drive chronic Quercus spp. decline but exacerbate tree weakening: a multi kingdom soil root continuum study in Chantilly forest

In European forests, several species of Quercus spp. are currently subject to major declines, defined as a slow deterioration of the tree caused by the interaction of abiotic and biotic factors, in which drought often plays an important role. The decline of holobionts, like oak trees, may have consequences that extend beyond the tree physiology. To better understand the interactions between chronic oak decline and oak associated microbial communities and their functional consequences, we analysed the bacterial, fungal and oomycetes communities in the bulk soil, the rhizosphere and the roots of pairs of declining and non-declining oaks in the Chantilly forest (France) by a metabarcoding approach. Chosen pairs of oaks were distributed on contrasted soil types to determine the influence of soil properties on the tree-microbe interactions during decline. Rhizospheric soil microbial enzymatic activities were also measured to assess the effect of oak decline on microbial-driven nutrient cycling activities. We observed a differential response of microbial communities to decline as a function of the kingdom. Bacteria were more sensitive to oak decline, particularly in the bulk soil and on podzol soil. By contrast, the composition of fungal communities was overall little impacted by oak decline, no matter the soil type, but their functional abilities may be partly impaired. Laccase and chitinase activities were reduced in the rhizospheric soil of declining oaks growing on brunisol and luvisol. Furthermore, the proportion of ectomycorrhizal fungi with high biomass exploration types were reduced by half in the roots of declining trees while the pathogenic Phytophthora cactorum and P. plurivora were more frequent. Altogether, our data suggest that chronic oak decline affects directly and indirectly the composition and functional abilities of oak associated microbial communities in a soil dependent manner.
