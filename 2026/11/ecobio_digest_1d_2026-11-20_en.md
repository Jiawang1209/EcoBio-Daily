### Depth-partitioned oxidation of atmospheric hydrogen, carbon monoxide and methane in a glacier forefield soil

Glacier forefields are model systems for pioneer ecosystem assembly under extreme oligotrophy. Their microbial communities carry widespread genomic potential to oxidize atmospheric trace gases, yet whether this potential is transcribed in situ, and by which lineages, remains largely untested. Here we combined depth-resolved profiling of hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO) and methane (CH4) at ten locations in the Wildstrubel glacier forefield, Swiss Alps, with mesocosm experiments, long-read metagenomics, and genome-resolved metatranscriptomics. Soils were consistent net sinks for atmospheric H2 and CH4, with fluxes ~2-fold and ~6-fold higher, respectively, in older than in younger terrain. H2 uptake was concentrated in the upper 20 cm, whereas CH4 uptake extended deeper. In the field, CO uptake was masked by apparent subsurface production, but in mesocosms lacking this source both CO and H2 were drawn below atmospheric concentrations. Although 57% of metagenome-assembled genomes encoded trace-gas oxidation enzymes, topsoil transcription was dominated by Group 1h [NiFe]-hydrogenases from a few Actinomycetota populations, in which the CO dehydrogenase coxL was transcribed 20- to 35-fold less. The second most transcribed marker was smoX, most closely related to C2-C6 alkane monooxygenases, suggesting the use of short-chain alkanes of possible subsurface origin. Mobile genetic elements were widely transcribed, matching host transcription, but a 104-kb plasmid-borne coxMSL operon was largely silent. Acetylene perturbation induced no detectable switching between trace-gas pathways. Together, these data suggest that trace-gas metabolism is partitioned across soil depth: atmospheric H2 scavenging dominates topsoil transcription, whereas methanotrophy and CO production occur deeper. Net surface fluxes, particularly for CO, therefore, underestimate gross trace-gas turnover.

### Defined bacterial consortia selectively enhance the degradation of recalcitrant crude-oil hydrocarbons in soil while reshaping soil microbial communities

Abstract Bioaugmentation offers a promising strategy for remediating petroleum-contaminated soils, but its effectiveness is often constrained by the persistence of recalcitrant hydrocarbon fractions. Here, we isolated 370 bacterial strains from oil-contaminated soils in Korea, China, and Vietnam, selecting eight representative strains to assemble three defined consortia: CB (four high-efficiency degraders), NS (four novel species candidates), and TC (CB + NS). In soil microcosms contaminated with 2.5% (w/w) crude oil, bioaugmentation selectively enhanced the degradation of recalcitrant hydrocarbons. After 30 days, removal of long-chain n-alkanes (C₂₅–C₃₃) reached 88.58–98.30% across consortia, compared to 61.35–78.44% in unbioaugmented controls. Pristane, phytane, and cyclic compounds were also significantly more effectively degraded in bioaugmented soils. 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing revealed temporal and treatment-dependent restructuring of the soil bacterial community. Several hydrocarbon-associated genera were enriched, while inoculated genera (including Gordonia , Mycolicibacterium , Stenotrophomonas , and Agrobacterium ) showed treatment-associated enrichment patterns. Notably, increasing consortium complexity from four to eight strains provided little additional benefit, indicating that greater inoculum diversity does not improve remediation performance. Overall, these findings demonstrate that defined bacterial consortia can selectively enhance recalcitrant hydrocarbon removal without requiring high consortium complexity.

### Vegetation cover shapes community structure and metabolic genetic potential of high-alpine soil microbiomes

Abstract Background Alpine permafrost-affected soils, particularly in the European Alps, remain poorly understood despite rapid warming and projected vegetation increase at higher elevations. We investigated how vegetation cover and physicochemical soil properties structure microbial communities across ten high-altitude alpine permafrost-affected sites. Results Soil parameters (pH, soil water content, temperature, and total C and N contents) varied significantly between sites and revealed pH and C: N ratio as key drivers modulated by vegetation cover, highlighting its indirect role in shaping soil environments. Microbial community composition differed significantly across sites, and functional gene profiles mirrored taxonomic patterns, indicating strong coupling between community structure and functional gene potential, with reduced functional redundancy. Microbial abundance increased with vegetation cover, whereas taxonomic and functional gene diversity plateaued at intermediate vegetation cover, suggesting that the presence of vegetation primarily enhances microbial abundance rather than diversity. Genome-resolved metagenomics revealed a shift from generalist-dominated communities in barren soils to increased prevalence of specialist taxa in vegetated soils. Functional gene abundances, particularly for C cycling, were higher in vegetated soils, while N and S cycling gene potentials were associated with soil temperature and water content. A microcosm experiment measuring ex situ CO₂ and CH₄ fluxes from incubated summer soils, identified vegetated soils as modest CO₂ sources. Vegetated soils exhibited lower, pH and C: N ratio, shaping a more abundant microbial community with more specialist members and higher C cycling gene potential. Conclusion Overall, our findings indicate that alpine greening is associated with microbial community turn-over via vegetation-mediated changes in soil properties, with implications for biogeochemical cycling and C emissions under climate warming.

### Sheep grazing within regenerative perennial systems accelerates soil carbon and nitrogen accrual

Abstract Background Global agricultural intensification and crop-livestock decoupling have reduced soil carbon and nitrogen stocks, weakening terrestrial carbon sinks and altering nutrient cycling. Integrating livestock into cropping systems is a cornerstone regenerative practice, with the potential to enhance carbon and nitrogen storage. However, on-farm research quantifying outcomes of stacked regenerative production models remains limited, and it is unclear how grazing, alone or combined with other practices, affects soil carbon and nitrogen accrual. We assessed carbon and nitrogen stocks (0–60 cm), including mineral-associated and particulate organic matter pools, microbial biomass and plant productivity across 49 California (USA) vineyards spanning conventional to regenerative management, with and without sheep grazing. We evaluated the relative contributions of management practices to carbon and nitrogen pools, accounting for spatial and edaphic predictors, and estimated accrual rates from vineyards with differing management histories (space-for-time substitution). Results Grazing emerged as the most influential management practice for increasing soil organic matter pool stocks, followed by reduced physical disturbance and permanent ground cover. Other regenerative practices, including organic amendments and avoidance of synthetic insecticides, showed positive but more variable associations with soil organic matter pool stocks. Grazing was strongly associated with approximately fivefold higher nitrogen accrual rates (+ 0.04 ± 0.01 Mg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), including plant-available nitrogen (+ 0.94 ± 0.30 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹), and more than threefold higher soil carbon accrual within regenerative management systems relative to conventional ungrazed systems (+ 0.38 ± 0.13 Mg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹). Grazing was also associated with greater microbial biomass and plant productivity, consistent with enhanced carbon and nitrogen cycling. Conclusions Under grazing, greater nitrogen availability and more robust plant and microbial communities likely reduced microbial nitrogen limitation while enhancing carbon sequestration and stabilization. Geography and edaphic properties constrained soil carbon and nitrogen accumulation. These patterns proved robust across geographically independent farms, highlighting the value of multi-farm datasets for climate-mitigation assessments. These results demonstrate that integrating grazing within regenerative perennial systems can help meet mitigation targets while building nitrogen stocks and reducing synthetic fertilizer reliance. This underscores the importance of research on commercial operations for translating site-specific results into scalable, climate-smart farming strategies.

### Rhizosphere metabolites link plant responses and microbial community restructuring under short-term continuous cropping of Angelica sinensis

Rhizosphere metabolites are key mediators of plant–soil interactions, yet how they link microbial community assembly to plant performance under short-term continuous cropping (STCC) remains poorly understood. This study combined greenhouse experiments with untargeted metabolomics and microbial amplicon sequencing to examine plant and rhizosphere responses to STCC in Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels. Plants grown in soils with longer cultivation histories showed reduced growth and elevated antioxidant-enzyme activities, accompanied by restructuring of the rhizosphere microbial community. Bacterial and fungal communities exhibited contrasting assembly patterns, and exploratory co-occurrence networks became less complex with increasing cropping history. Metabolite composition, rather than chemodiversity, was significantly associated with bacterial diversity and community composition. Seventeen plant trait-associated candidate metabolite features showed contrasting associations with specific microbial genera and predicted bacterial functions; however, these associations did not remain significant after adjusting for cropping treatment. Together, these findings identify candidate metabolite features that link rhizosphere chemical variation to plant and microbial responses and provide a foundation for testing chemical–microbial contributions to plant–soil feedback under continuous cropping.

### Climate-selected microbiomes as evolutionary solutions and beyond conventional “inputs”

Plant-associated microbes, collectively known as the phytomicrobiome, strongly influence plant growth, nutrient acquisition, and stress resilience. However, they are often viewed as external agricultural inputs rather than dynamic components of plant adaptation. This review introduces the concept of climate-selected microbiomes as evolutionary solutions shaped by long-term natural and anthropogenic stress. We synthesize evidence that unmanaged habitats, including native ecosystems, urban and roadside soils, saline and contaminated environments, and extreme climatic regions, serve as reservoirs of stress-adapted microorganisms. Continuous exposure to drought, salinity, nutrient limitation, temperature fluctuations, and pollution acts as an evolutionary filter, enriching microbial traits associated with functional flexibility, bio-signaling, nutrient mobilization, and contaminant transformation. These naturally selected microbial communities may support plant stress tolerance and phenotypic plasticity more effectively than microbiomes from intensively managed soils. We also discuss how climate-driven rhizosphere assembly, fine-scale microbial evolution, and stress-induced changes in microbial secretomes can generate transferable benefits for crop performance. Leveraging naturally evolving microbial pools may provide a low-input and scalable alternative to conventional biofertilizers and chemical-based practices, while reducing risks linked to poorly adapted inoculants. Reframing plant microbiomes as climate-refined evolutionary assets can support sustainable agriculture, ecosystem restoration, and food security under accelerating climate change.
