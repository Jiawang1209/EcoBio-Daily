### SIP-enabled multi-omics reveals soil microbiome responses to drought and rehydration

Abstract The activity of the soil microbiome, and its balance of anabolic (organic C consuming) and catabolic (CO 2 -releasing) reactions, determines the magnitude and direction of soil carbon fluxes. Over half a century of research has revealed that soil water dynamics are key controllers of microbial activity. With increasing hydroclimate volatility expected across many regions of the Earth, there is a greater need to describe and quantify microbial responses to drought and rehydration cycles. In this study, we conducted rainfall exclusion experiments at two archetypical Mediterranean-type field sites. After rainfall exclusion and subsequent soil rehydration, we applied a SIP-enabled, multi-omics methodology to generate a multi-faceted case study of microbial growth, greenhouse gas fluxes, and the forms of carbon that drive both. Our results indicate that rehydration increases microbial anabolic processes by orders of magnitude, shifting cell generation times from years to days within just minutes. High-intensity drought increases the lag period before microbial growth resumes, but both stable-isotope probing and metagenomic inference agree that microbial communities exhibit greater capacity for rapid growth following drought stress. Furthermore, significant shifts in the soil metabolome are observed following drought and rehydration, implicating specific osmolytes as key to the microbial response and indicating metabolite diversity as a key modulator of microbiome functioning. Together, our results provide constraints on microbial activity rates in soil and mechanisms underpinning microbial responses to drought and rewetting. These findings motivate further research into microbial responses under increasingly volatile hydroclimate regimes and downstream contributions to the global carbon cycle. Significance Statement Soil is a major global store and source of carbon. The microbiome determine the fate of soil organic carbon, and the microbiome is ultimately controlled by soil water dynamics. Early, innovative experiments by H.F. Birch defined “The Birch Effect” – the observation that soils emit CO 2 following drying and subsequent rehydration. However, it remains unclear when, and to what magnitude, soil microorganisms are actively growing following this rehydration, and what biological mechanisms explain the observed CO 2 pulse. In this work, we apply an array of methodologies to address this question, describing rates of microbial growth during drought and rewetting. Our results provide crucial insights into how soil microbiomes will respond to increasing hydroclimate volatility across the globe.

### Chemical warfare in the rice plant rhizosphere: Diverse Pseudomonas antagonize the green alga Chlamydomonas

Algae and bacteria form dynamic partnerships that shape diverse ecosystems. The model green microalga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii is often found in paddy rice fields, where the diversity and functional characteristics of its bacterial partners remain largely unexplored. Here, we isolated 12 bacterial strains from the rice rhizosphere and its surrounding soil. Phylogenetic analysis based on 16S rRNA gene sequences revealed that eight isolates belong to the genus Pseudomonas . Three Pseudomonas rhizosphere strains exhibited antagonistic effects against C. reinhardtii , negatively impacting its growth, pigmentation and cell morphology. Close physical associations between the three antagonistic Pseudomonas strains and C. reinhardtii were observed in liquid cultures. Whole genome sequencing identified the antagonistic strains as Pseudomonas alloputida , Pseudomonas tohonis , and Pseudomonas asiatica . The three bacterial genomes encode several factors well-known for mediating chemical warfare. Notably, P. tohonis bears a gene cluster encoding the phenolic toxin, 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG). DAPG inhibits the growth and alters the cell morphology of C. reinhardtii . These findings expand our understanding of the diversity of bacterial partners associated with C. reinhardtii and highlight the role of Pseudomonas rice rhizosphere-associated bacteria as potent green algal antagonists. They harbor a reservoir of common functional traits that negatively influence green algal population dynamics.

### Complementary nutrient-mobilizing traits in herbal rhizosphere bacteria support a multi-strain bioinoculant

Abstract Excessive use of synthetic fertilizers has driven interest in microbial alternatives for sustainable agriculture, and herbal plant rhizospheres host distinctive microbial communities with bio-fertilization potential. Eleven bacterial isolates representing nine species ( Priestia megaterium , Priestia aryabhattai , Bacillus cereus , Bacillus xiamenensis , Enterobacter chuandaensis , Enterobacter cloacae , Burkholderia seminalis , Burkholderia multivorans and Chromobacterium violaceum ) and three Trichoderma endophytes (two T. yunnanense and one T. simmonsii ) were isolated from the rhizosphere of Barleria lupulina . They were identified by 16S rRNA or internal transcribed spacer (ITS) gene sequencing, sequence similarity searches and, for the bacteria, phylogenetic analysis. Growth on nitrogen-free medium was widespread, and all 14 isolates produced ammonia. Phosphate and potassium solubilization were restricted to B. seminalis and B. multivorans , with solubilization indices of 2.45 and 2.16 on Pikovskaya agar and 2.38 and 2.83 on Aleksandrov agar, respectively. Zinc solubilization occurred in B. cereus , E. chuandaensis , E. cloacae and B. xiamenensis (indices 2.09–2.57). As no single isolate showed all four traits, a three-strain consortium ( E. chuandaensis , B. seminalis and E. cloacae ) was selected to cover nitrogen fixation and phosphate, potassium and zinc solubilization, with redundancy for zinc. Isolates with confirmed nitrogen fixation were prioritized over the higher-index but weakly nitrogen-fixing B. cereus . Herbal rhizospheres are therefore a source of multi-trait plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) for bioinoculant development.
